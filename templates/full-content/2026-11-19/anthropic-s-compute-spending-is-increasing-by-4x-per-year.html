Anthropic spent nearly $10 billion on compute and infrastructure in the third quarter of 2026, or close to $40 billion on an annualized basis, up from just $433 million in 2023. By comparison, OpenAI appears to have roughly tripled its compute spending annually and forecasts similar growth for 2026, though data for OpenAI is sparser.

Most of Anthropic and OpenAI’s compute spending goes to cloud partners such as Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and SpaceX, which rent compute capacity to Anthropic and OpenAI. Much of Anthropic’s compute spend growth this year is attributable to SpaceX: Anthropic paid SpaceX around $5 billion for compute in the third quarter of 2026, roughly half of Anthropic’s total compute spending that quarter.

We compile reports and disclosures about Anthropic and OpenAI’s spending on compute over time, and convert these figures into spending run rates to estimate how quickly each company has ramped up their compute spending over the past several years.

Data

For Anthropic, we use compute spending figures from the company’s Form S-1, filed in advance of its IPO. The S-1 disclosed “compute and infrastructure” spending for the seven calendar quarters from 2025 through Q3 2026, as well as annual totals in 2023 and 2024. It also disclosed spending commitments of $447.5 billion, “substantially all” related to compute and infrastructure, from September 2026 through the end of 2030, or around $100 billion per year.

For OpenAI, we source compute spend from media reports. OpenAI President Greg Brockman testified in May 2026 that the company expects to spend $50 billion on compute this year, but we do not have any non-forecasted figures for OpenAI in 2026.

Because these spending figures are a mix of quarterly and annual data, they need to be converted to annualized run rates for direct comparison.

Analysis

All of Anthropic and OpenAI’s compute spend figures are period-based totals for a given year or quarter. To compare a mix of annual and quarterly data, we convert them into estimated annualized spending rates. For quarterly numbers, this means multiplying the spending amount by four. We also need to attribute the spending rates for each period to an exact date. We assign each spending estimate a date by assuming that spending grew at a steady exponential rate between reporting periods.

We then fit growth rates to these points via a log-linear regression. We derive 90% confidence intervals analytically using standard errors.

Most of Anthropic’s compute is used internally for R&D and other purposes, not to serve external customers. Anthropic’s S-1 discloses its customer inference spending as “cost of revenue,” which is “substantially all” compute:

  • In 2025, Anthropic’s cost of revenue was $2.773 billion, or 38% of its $7.326 billion in total compute spending.
  • In the first 9 months of 2026, this share decreased to just 26%, or $4.812 billion in cost of revenue against $18.8 billion in compute spend.

By comparison, media reports indicate that around 50% of OpenAI’s compute spending was allocated to customer inference in 2025, up from roughly one-third in 2024.

In terms of future growth, Anthropic has also already contractually committed to $447.5 billion in future compute spending through 2030 per its S-1. This would be roughly another doubling from the current rate, so it is not clear that Anthropic’s future compute spending will grow at anywhere near the 4x annual growth of the past three years. Meanwhile, OpenAI plans to spend around $750 billion on compute over the same period.

Assumptions and limitations

  • OpenAI’s compute spending figures come from media reporting, not from disclosed financial statements, so there is more uncertainty in the underlying accuracy and interpretation of the numbers.
  • Dollar spending cannot be straightforwardly converted into other metrics of compute capacity, such as floating point operations per second or watts of power.
  • While it is safe to assume that a large majority of these companies’ compute spending is for capacity directly supporting AI research and the training and inference of AI models, we do not know exactly how this spending breaks down between AI accelerator capacity, dedicated CPU capacity related to training or inference, or general cloud compute capacity for other parts of the technology stack.
