Long-term, there is little incentive for self-driving car companies to prioritize the safety of road users like cyclists. They can eat any amount of fine payments. They can bring any amount of legal influence to bear. It is simply not wise to allow this kind of power imbalance between road users! I think the threat of a tech company becoming a road user is, long-term, greater than the benefit of automatic driving.

This is a really good point I hadn't really considered myself, although I also generally oppose "autonomous" vehicle deployment by corporations for a few related but distinct reasons.

The first one is that I think safety claims from companies like Waymo need to be treated with extreme skepticism; tech companies lie basically all of the time about basically everything, so they're probably lying about this. Don't tell me they wouldn't do pivot to video with people's lives.

But beyond outright lying, many of the claims are just misleading.

The crux of the article is a comparison between the crash rate of Waymos and the crash rate of "all drivers", which as far as I can tell from Waymo's own descriptions of their methodology, means all drivers in US counties that Waymo operates in.

These comparisons inherently require statistical adjustment to approximate an apples to apples comparison, because Waymos are just not driving the same routes that human drivers are; in particular, they do very little highway driving, which is significantly more dangerous than city driving—per the US DoT, rural driving has about a 50% higher fatality rate per distance traveled.

Waymos are also, you know, Waymos—small minivans and crossovers, all electric, all pretty new, all very inherently safe. We're comparing this to the median American car, which is an SUV with a fetish for rolling over or a pickup truck with a fetish for running over children.

Waymo has published peer-reviewed papers about their safety record—all in the same journal. It's worth questioning whether anyone with a real incentive to interrogate their methodology is in a position to do so. Everyone in power is kind of broadly incentivized to go along with Waymo's claims. Auto makers want a new market for cars; policy makers want a convenient tech solution to road safety problems that doesn't require them doing work or the state spending money. The idea that self-driving is extremely safe is extremely convenient, which is itself a reason to be skeptical.

It's also worth saying that by far the most convenient way for Waymo to exaggerate their safety record is this: Waymos are not always operating autonomously; they are often being guided by remote operators, whom the company will not refer to as "drivers" for liability reasons. Whatever Waymo says about when exactly or how often those remote operators are in control is, of course, something that Waymo gets to disclose or lie about with total impunity; it's not independently verifiable.

The way Waymo talks about road safety is also designed to induce in the reader and overestimation of the safety gain from Waymos. A large proportion of road accidents is caused by either drunk driving or speeding. If you're walking into a rideshare or taxi vehicle with a presumably sober driver who isn't going to drive recklessly, you are already significantly safer than the average person on the road, so the broad comparison Waymo is making is exaggerating what the safety gap is in specific circumstances.

The conversation also inherently marginalizes questions of what things we could do to promote road safety that are not giving over control of the road to one of the world's most evil corporations. How would Waymos compare to human drivers who were being more effectively induced to drive safely through traffic calming and other infrastructure that generally isn't being adopted by cities in the Americas?

However, all of this is just pointing out that Waymo is almost definitely exaggerating their numbers and presenting them in a very rosy light; even so, you might look at the actual numbers and say "well, it's such a huge difference, there's definitely a meaningful benefit there."

Here's the problem with that. One reason Waymos are very safe because they drive extremely conservatively. For this reason, they are also slow and will sometimes get "stuck" when they can't determine a safe way forward. This approach to driving is a good idea for Waymo right now, when they're trying to gain regulatory acceptance for their vehicles in more and more cities.

But let's say Waymo captures significant market share. Let's say a large city, or several large cities, actually do become meaningfully dependent on Waymo for transportation. Being slow makes Waymos uncompetitive against other modes, and it makes the service costlier to operate.

On a self-driving vehicle operated by a corporation, road safety is a dial. It's something Alphabet just gets to turn up or down depending on their goals. If Waymo ever becomes established as a significant transportation provider, very soon after there'll be a meeting in a dark room in Mountain View where they'll decide, for all of those cities they're operating in, what an acceptable level of road fatalities is. What an acceptable level of cyclist-car collisions is. That number probably looks pretty similar to the number that policymakers throughout the US, Canada, and Latin America tend to think is acceptable right now, given the degree to which cars are tacitly allowed to kill people. They'll turn that dial until it hits that "acceptable" equilibrium, because safety costs them money.

There are no technical solutions to political problems. Waymo cannot solve road safety, categorically; it can only shift the locus of policymaking about road safety, away from the democratic control of state institutions and into an unaccountable, dishonest, and fundamentally evil corporation. In the process, the seduction of easy technical solutions can only harm progress towards real policy change.

To think Waymo is a solution to road safety is a trap. You are talking about buying safety from an entity that has zero commitment to human life.
