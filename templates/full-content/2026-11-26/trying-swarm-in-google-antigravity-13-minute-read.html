Kevin Hou, who leads product engineering on Google Antigravity, recommended /swarm paired with Gemini 4 Neon and Orchestrator right after a quota reset.

I had seen /swarm mentioned when Gemini 4 Carbon rolled out, but I hadn’t run it myself yet. So I opened the Antigravity desktop app on this site’s repository (flaviocopes.com) and put it through a real audit.

The main question I wanted to answer was simple: what do you need /swarm for that one good agent can’t do on its own?

Orchestrator in Antigravity

Before running /swarm, I picked Orchestrator in the model selector.

In Antigravity, Orchestrator pairs two models inside one conversation: a larger planner model explores the codebase, writes the plan, and checks progress, while a faster executor model handles searches, file edits, builds, and tests.

Here is how Antigravity splits the two roles on Google AI Pro and Google AI Ultra plans:

Role What it handles Supported models
Orchestrator (planner) Exploring the codebase, writing the plan, reviewing progress Gemini 4 Neon (default), Gemini 4 Carbon, Gemini 4 Argon, Claude Opus 5.5
Executor Searches, file edits, running builds and tests Gemini 4 Hydrogen (default), Claude Sonnet 5.5

There is one small UI quirk when you open the model menu under the prompt box. Hovering Orchestrator opens a submenu with only the four planner models. Hydrogen isn’t listed in that submenu because it is the executor, already selected by default under Settings -> Models -> Orchestrator Settings.

Running /swarm on flaviocopes.com

To give /swarm a real job that divides cleanly across separate parts of a repo, I asked for a strictly read-only audit of three areas of flaviocopes.com plus a verifier to check every claim:

/swarm Audit three areas of flaviocopes.com with separate read-only workers: (1) scripts/check-internal-links.mjs and recent drafts for broken internal links, (2) scripts/audit-images.mjs and public/images/ for oversized or unused assets, (3) scripts/build-site.mjs and package.json for the biggest build-time bottlenecks. Have a verifier cross-check every reported file path and line number, then post one ranked summary on the board.

When you run /swarm, your main conversation becomes @coordinator. It spent about a minute inspecting the repo, spawned five workers (@links-auditor, @images-auditor, @build-auditor, @verifier, and a quiet @monitor to watch for stalls), and returned an Open the Swarm UI button while the auditors ran in parallel.

That right-hand Swarm pane has four tabs: Overview, Board, Members, and Graph.

The Board tab is the shared message feed where the entire team communicates in public. Posts #1 through #4 are the coordinator’s briefs to each worker, spelling out what they own, what they must not touch, and how to report back.

The Graph tab and what @verifier caught

The Graph tab lets you inspect the swarm in two ways: a Mentions graph showing who messaged whom, and a Hive view with pixel-art bees inside hexagons and a replay scrubber across every post.

The Hive view is fun to watch, and the replay scrubber at the bottom is where I spotted the most useful part of the whole run. @verifier had checked @links-auditor’s first report against the actual files in the repository, caught two inaccuracies, and sent it back to be fixed before the coordinator wrote the final report.

Once all three tracks passed verification, the coordinator posted the 10-item ranked summary to the board, killed the four workers, and printed the same summary in the main chat.

The top findings were real bugs and bottlenecks I hadn’t noticed:

  1. Whole-site cache busting in the Astro build. src/content.config.ts loads 6,232 content files, and figuresVersion hashes all 330 files in src/figures/ into one markdown plugin option. Editing a single figure invalidates cached HTML across all 6,232 entries.
  2. Throttled builds and a 1.3 GB copy. astro.config.mjs caps build.concurrency at 2 across roughly 8,721 output artifacts, while copying 1.3 GB from public/ on every build.
  3. 233 MiB of unused or shadowed assets. public/images/ holds 7,418 files, including 1,079 completely unreferenced files.
  4. A broken image audit script. scripts/audit-images.mjs walks .mp4 files alongside images, and misses 3,021 of the 3,646 images wider than 1600px.
  5. Blind spots in the internal link checker. scripts/lib/site-routes.mjs only reads src/pages/apps/*.md and misses 79 live paid apps.

When /swarm beats a single agent (and when it doesn’t)

For most daily coding, you don’t need /swarm. One agent on Orchestrator (Neon + Hydrogen) is simpler, finishes small edits faster, and uses far less quota.

Here is where /swarm earns the extra tokens:

  1. Fresh-context verification. Asking one agent to double-check its own work in the same conversation rarely catches mistakes because it is anchored to its own assumptions.
  2. Avoiding context pollution on wide tasks. Auditing links, scanning images, and profiling a build pipeline in one thread fills a single context window with unrelated noise.
  3. Cutting wall-clock time on independent slices. When multiple workers edit code simultaneously, the coordinator can give each author its own working copy of the repository.
  4. Testing parallel hypotheses. When you don’t know which optimization or root cause will pay off, separate workers can investigate different theories at once.

And here is where a single agent is still the better choice:

  • Tightly coupled edits across two or three files that depend on each other.
  • One deep, hard bug, like a subtle race condition.
  • Small everyday tasks. Every swarm member is its own agent, so a swarm burns through your quota much faster.

How I would use it

For normal day-to-day work, I stay on a single agent. I would pull out /swarm for two specific jobs:

  • Periodic repo audits where read-only workers check links, images, and build performance in parallel while @verifier throws out false positives.
  • Wide, mechanical changes across isolated files, especially with @verifier pinned to a second model to catch weakened assertions.
