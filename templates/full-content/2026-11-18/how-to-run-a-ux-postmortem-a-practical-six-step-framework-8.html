Design critiques help us answer a question before a product ships: Do we think this is the right solution? Once a feature launches, we rarely go back to analyze how our decisions actually played out. And even when we do, the outcomes may not always tell the whole story. Thoughtful design decisions could lead to poor outcomes due to changing user needs. Meanwhile, weaker decisions might succeed unexpectedly. Reviewing a project retrospectively can help us understand the difference between making a good decision and getting a good result.

As a senior UX designer, I’ve spent years in design critiques discussing how users might behave, which workflows will make the most sense, and where they might get confused. Once the design ships, the team usually moves on to the next problem. We might look at adoption metrics or support tickets, but we rarely revisit the original design decisions and compare them with what actually happened.

Some of the most useful design lessons I’ve learned have come from revisiting shipped work and realizing that my assumptions were either wrong or more nuanced than I expected.

In this article, we’ll explore why design teams predict outcomes before launch but rarely revisit them afterward. We’ll look at treating design decisions as hypotheses and what I’ve learned from revisiting shipped work. I’ll also share a simple framework for running UX postmortems without turning them into another heavy process for the team.

A UX postmortem, also called a design postmortem, is a post-launch design review. It compares design assumptions with real user behavior. A broader UX retrospective may also examine teamwork and process. Here, the focus is on what shipped work teaches us about our design decisions.

Key Takeaways

  • Revisit the reasoning: Compare the assumptions behind a design with what users actually did after launch, rather than judging the decision by its outcome alone.
  • Use multiple sources of evidence: Combine analytics, research, support feedback, and session recordings to understand behavior and identify what remains uncertain.
  • Keep the learning reusable: Run a lightweight UX postmortem, capture surprises, and turn observations into lessons the team can apply to future work.

We spend more time predicting than learning

Part of the problem is that the reasoning behind those decisions can get lost over time. When a product decision influences the design, we document it in our Figma files. This keeps the reasoning accessible beyond a Zoom recording and helps us understand why certain choices were made. A record of the context and reasoning helps someone new pick up a design. It also helps when a project gets shelved and revisited months later. It also gives us a starting point for understanding our decisions after launch.

Most design critiques happen before launch. We discuss the problem, review concepts, challenge assumptions, and try to predict how users will respond. Mature UX teams already use analytics, usability research, and customer feedback to understand how their products are performing. Once our customers start using the product, we finally have evidence about whether those predictions were right. But what I think many teams are missing is the connection between those findings and the decisions that led them there. Product analytics might show that a feature is underused, support tickets might reveal confusion, or research might uncover an unexpected workflow. We address the immediate issue and move on without revisiting why we made the original decision.

Sometimes, we get so caught up in work and priorities that we forget to look back. On one project, we hid several secondary controls to simplify a workflow. We expected most users to follow the same path. After launch, we noticed experienced users repeatedly opening the advanced options. They needed those controls to handle less common scenarios. The solution wasn’t simply to put everything back. What initially looked like an interface problem was actually a gap in our understanding of how different users approached the task.

You can easily miss that lesson if you’re only looking at whether the feature is performing well. The more useful question is what the outcome tells us about the assumptions behind the design. That’s where looking back becomes useful because we can compare what we expected with what actually happened.

Revisiting shipped work changed how I think about design

I’ve also had hypotheses that didn’t play out as I expected. On one workflow, we debated adding an extra review step because it felt like it would slow users down. We ultimately kept it because it gave users a chance to confirm their changes before committing them. We noticed that users were completing the review step and continuing through the workflow rather than dropping off at that point.

I realized I had been treating the extra click as friction without considering what it provided in return. At first, I took this as a sign that the extra step wasn’t creating as much friction as we’d expected. But the completion rate alone wasn’t enough to tell us whether users found the extra step helpful or were simply clicking through it. To better understand that, we’d need to look more closely at how users interacted with the review step or hear directly from them. When I revisit shipped work now, I separate three things. First, what did we believe before launch? Second, what does the evidence tell us now? Finally, what would we change if we were designing it today? That last part is especially important.

Treating decisions as hypotheses

One thing that’s changed how I think about design is treating decisions as hypotheses rather than conclusions. Every workflow makes assumptions about what users need, what they’ll understand, and how they’ll behave. A critique can help us challenge those assumptions, but we won’t know how well they hold up until people actually use the product.

To evaluate our hypotheses, we need to document the reasoning behind our decisions. Capture the problem, the evidence supporting our approach, and the outcomes we expect for users. This way, we have answers to revisit and anchor to later on.

On another project, we cleaned up a map interface by grouping several actions together. They seemed to belong to the same task. After launch, we noticed users were separating those actions and moving between different parts of the workflow instead. Their behavior showed that our mental model didn’t fully match theirs.

But this observation wasn’t enough to conclude that our direction was wrong. Another explanation for their behavior could be that they were unfamiliar with the layout, the actions weren’t discoverable, or different types of users needed different workflows. To explain the behavior, we’d need to understand what users were trying to accomplish. We’d also need to check its frequency and whether it affected task completion. The useful part is understanding what that behavior tells us about the decision we made.

Borrow the learning, not the process

Engineering teams have long used postmortems to understand what happened after a system goes live. I think there’s something useful for design teams to borrow here, but not necessarily the process itself. A design postmortem makes time to understand what happened after a decision and which factors contributed to the outcome. A design might underperform for multiple reasons, such as incorrect assumptions, technical limitations, changing requirements, or simply lack of visibility to users.

I don’t think every shipped feature needs a formal meeting or a detailed document. That would quickly become another process for designers to maintain. The depth of the retrospective should depend on how much the decision impacts users, how uncertain we were about our assumptions, and whether the same decision could influence future work.

A simple framework for UX postmortems

A useful postmortem doesn’t need to review the entire project. It should focus on what we expected, what happened, and what we learned. You don’t need a lengthy retrospective or a detailed report.

1. Document your decisions and hypotheses

To run an effective postmortem post-launch, your team should have a clear understanding of the problem that you’re solving before launching. Document the assumptions and evidence behind your design decisions. Explain how those decisions might affect users. It can also be useful to write out your hypotheses around how you expect users’ behaviour to change afterwards.

2. Revisit the original assumptions

Start by going back to what the team believed before launch. Which problem did you think you were solving? How did you expect users to behave? What assumptions did the design depend on? Which elements did you intentionally simplify, prioritize, or leave out?

3. Look at what actually happened

Then look for evidence that supports or challenges those assumptions. This could be product analytics, usability research, support tickets, customer feedback, session recordings, or conversations with customer-facing teams. Don’t rely on a single metric. You’re trying to understand not just what happened, but why.

4. Identify the surprises

Look for anything that happened differently than expected, both good and bad. Maybe users found a workflow easier to learn than anticipated or they ignored a feature the team thought would be valuable. Surprises can make us question how well we understood the problem or if we thought of it in a different way.

5. Turn observations into principles

Don’t stop at documenting what happened. Ask what you can take from it and apply to the next project. For example, “users kept opening the advanced controls” is an observation. “Design simple defaults without removing the flexibility experienced users need” is a lesson you can apply to other workflows.

6. Make the learning reusable

Finally, capture the lesson somewhere the team will actually find it. A simple page with four things is often enough: the original assumption, what actually happened, what surprised us, and what we’d do differently next time.

Conclusion

Design critiques help us make better predictions. Postmortems help us get better at making those predictions. But we won’t always learn the right lessons from looking back at our work. Often, confirmation bias can get in the way of learning from our past decisions. Designers should be open to questioning their own interpretations and try to see things from different perspectives.

If you want to try this with your own team, start small. Pick one recently shipped feature and compare what you expected to happen with what actually happened. Look for one assumption that held up, one surprise, and one thing you’d approach differently today. Then capture that lesson somewhere the team can find it.
