Raising the ceiling must be earned

There is a phrase: raise the floor and ceiling. This means improving both the baseline and maximum potential at the same time. It’s been used in many applications such as the potential or teams. Recently, it’s been applied to Design in the age of design; a phrase Figma CEO Dylan CEO repeated at Config.

At UXDX San Francisco this week, I had the pleasure of self-moderating a conversation with Josh Clemm, VP of Software Engineering at Figma.

One of the questions asked was if taste will remain a human element? As a designer, you may expect my answer, but I had a controversial take. Some AI has better taste than some humans.

If you think AI Slop is bad, have you seen Human Slop?

In I, Robot, Detective Spooner, played by Will Smith, questions the robot Sonny (voiced by Alan Tudyk) about what separates humans from machines. He asks whether a robot can write a symphony or turn a canvas into a masterpiece. Sonny answers with a simple question: “Can you?” Spooner has no answer. It’s easy to point to great art as proof of what humans can do. It’s a different thing to make it yourself.

Using AI tools is not an entitlement to elevated craft. The output is only as good as the person providing the input. AI will only be as good as the historical context and the prompter. To know you’re a good prompter, one must know how to critique the outputs and give better direction. Without it, things become AI slop; a phenomenon that is less about the output rather it is people’s tolerance to the output quality.

The ceiling can’t be raised while sitting on the floor

To push the bounds of your craft, you need some understanding of where those bounds are. That comes from practice: making things, noticing where they fall short, and learning enough to make the next version better. New tools can help you move through that process, but the fundamentals still give you something to build on.

Vibe coding can get you from an idea to a working prototype in minutes. That’s a meaningful head start. But when something breaks, or a customer uses it in a way you didn’t expect, you need to understand what’s happening beneath the interface. Where does the data go? What happens when a request fails? Does the code solve the problem, or did it just make the error disappear?

You don’t need to master every part of software development before making something. Building can be how you learn. Take one piece of the prototype and follow how it works. Change it, see what breaks, and try to explain why. The risk is getting so used to a working result that you stop asking how it works. Putting off those questions gets expensive when other people start depending on what you’ve made.

The same applies to design. You can generate dozens of layouts and still struggle to explain why one works better than another. Knowing how hierarchy, typography, and interaction shape an experience helps you critique what the tool gives you and give it better direction. With practice, you can begin to see where the familiar approach falls short—and have a reason to try something else.

Elevation of craft comes in many forms

There are different approaches to get to the ceiling. I believe there are multiple contributors for the craft bar to be raised aside from raw skill. This is why I dedicated my career to work on tools that revolutionize the internet and empower. When I was at Webflow from 2019-2023, Application Layer AI experiences were still very early, and most code was still hand-written. I spoke to so many customers who told me Webflow was what allowed them to Break the Code Barrier. The syntax of front-end code wasn’t intuitive for them. Using Webflow, they were empowered to design and build visually. They used no-code to know code.

Replit was a similar story. The number one detractor from people learning to code is not programming itself but the ability to even get the development environment in the first place. The Xcode provisioning profile setup killed so many dreams of people wanting to build iOS applications.

Augmenting people is a way to raise them to the ceiling. Having taste without the hands and technical skills does not guarantee you will be a good designer. At the same time, being good at technical skills and not having a sense of what to create or the meaning behind it also does not make you a good designer. You need to be able to balance both. I’ve long said I don’t care how they get to shipping as long as they ship. This might be through raw skill, empowerment, or leverage that gets them to the ceiling.

Bringing the best of your craft is the only way to move the ceiling, and there is a lot to do to be the best at your craft. It requires both repetition in your own excellence and opening the aperture of what you’ve done. In art school I had the misconception about Salvador Dali being only a surrealist painter. When I lived in Barcelona, some friends took me to Figueres, Dali’s home town. There I saw I was so wrong about the Spanish artist. In the casa had technical drawings, paintings in the style of the old masters, and some of the most intricate jewelry and metal smithing work.

Many of the best artists who abstracted away reality were able to create in such a manner first. There is a similar lesson here for software and brand designers using AI. The best ones already have a sense of how to accelerate and push what is already known to them to invent new originalities. When you don’t know what you’re doing, there is risk of unknowingly producing slop, whether intentional or not.

Raising the floor allows more people to participate in a craft. Some may choose to be hobbyists and others may blaze a path into the craft professionally. However, raising the ceiling is a collective effort an entire craft industry must do together. It’s because our collective pushing of it is the only way we can push what we have collectively done throughout history. For LLMs, they do this without context and require us.

In the agentic era, raising the ceiling of your craft must be earned.
