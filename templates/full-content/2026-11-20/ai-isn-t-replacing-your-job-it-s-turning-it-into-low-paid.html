The gig economy has arrived for cardiologists. Recruiting platform Outlier, run by Scale AI, advertises offers of up to $180 an hour for physicians in the U.S. to design complex cardiac scenarios, review artificial intelligence-generated reports, and rank model outputs. The site offers up to $7.50 an hour for Tagalog work in the Philippines for the same job, and up to $12.50 an hour for Swahili in Tanzania.

Companies like Scale AI first outsourced data work to wherever labor was cheapest. Now, they are recruiting physicians, lawyers, and Ph.D.s to generate and annotate data, rank model answers, and verify outputs. Higher credentials can raise the advertised rate, but the platform still controls payment and working conditions.

They begin by paying well to build a workforce, then they cut the rates and move on.

I’ve spent the last six years interviewing the people who do this work. Most were in Venezuela, where economic collapse has left a large, educated population online and out of options. One of them, Rogelio, taught computer science until the economic crisis and the pandemic left his family without work. His 13-year-old son discovered they could earn money labeling images of cars and roads on the platforms Hive and Scale AI’s Remotasks, using a laptop the Hugo Chávez government had issued to schoolchildren a decade earlier. The boy made 18 cents for a few hours of work. Rogelio joined him and made just over $2 on his first day.

For a while, the work paid. A task called Beast Mode where workers labeled objects in people’s homes paid more than any other work the family had done. In December 2020, with schools closed and the whole family working, it brought in $20 in a single day. It was the most they would ever make. By early 2021, Beast Mode had disappeared, and their earnings fell below Hive’s $5 weekly payout threshold.

This is how many platforms operate: They begin by paying well to build a workforce, then they cut the rates and move on. Remotasks ended its hourly program in Venezuela in 2021 and made a splash in Kenya the same year. In March 2024, it discontinued operations in Kenya, Nigeria and Pakistan. Scale AI attributed the closures to “enhanced security protocols,” and said notices had gone out three weeks ahead but later acknowledged that Kenyan workers never received them “due to operational errors.” Most workers who spoke to Rest of World at the time said the platform simply froze them out.

The pattern has a name, and it’s older than AI: extractivism. It is a system that takes resources and value from people and places for accumulation elsewhere, on terms set by the buyer rather than the worker. The term also stands for what is taken and what is left behind. The crisis that put Rogelio’s family online was the residue of an oil economy that built little else.

An oil field stays put, while a platform can send the next batch of tasks to workers somewhere else.

One woman I interviewed in Venezuela drew a pension from the state oil company. By 2021, it was worth about $1 a month, which she said couldn’t buy half a dozen eggs. She was labeling data on Hive to make up the difference, but that income was even less secure. An oil field stays put, while a platform can send the next batch of tasks to workers somewhere else.

AI is usually described as built on extracted data: authors’ books, artists’ works, all used to train models. But the extraction has another layer: the new examples, evaluations, and corrections commissioned to improve those models. That material has to be created by someone, for a fee, which is set by the platform — whether it is $2 for the teacher in Venezuela or up to $180 an hour for a cardiologist in the U.S.

Outlier’s terms let the company delay or suspend payment during an investigation of a worker’s tasks and withhold “some or all amounts accrued” where it finds a violation “in the company’s sole determination.” The company may also deactivate an account “immediately without prior notice for any reason.” The same language appeared word for word in the Remotasks terms that Rogelio’s family worked under in 2021.

Earlier this year, Josh Dzieza reported on the laid-off lawyers, scientists, and screenwriters now filling these platforms. Of the more than 30 workers he interviewed, nearly all described the same arc: Demands increased, target times shrank, and pay fell as a project went on. One designer was tagging videos for Meta at $21 an hour when the project ended for “scope changes.” Days later, the same job reappeared under a new name at $16 an hour — the same sequence that Rogelio’s family went through five years earlier.

Of course, workers can leave, and many do. But the ones who stay are often those who can’t leave. One woman I interviewed was confined to her home during the Covid-19 pandemic by a health condition and couldn’t risk losing the job. “This is my only source of income,” she told me.

The rate can change suddenly, the task can disappear without warning, and they have no say over any of it.

None of this is unknown within the industry. In a 2024 meeting convened by the nonprofit Partnership on AI and the Fairwork project which rates platform companies, I sat with representatives of AI companies and the outsourcing firms that supply them. The AI companies said they didn’t employ the data workers; the outsourcing firms said their operations were determined by the prices the AI companies expected. Everyone agreed pay should be better, but when the question of representation came up, the room went quiet as no workers were present.

Workers have still acted. In March 2023, a judge in Kenya temporarily halted the dismissal of 260 Facebook content moderators, prevented Meta from moving their work to another contractor, and barred Meta and contractor Majorel from blacklisting workers. As workers fought in Kenya, Meta moved the work to West Africa.

American courts are also grappling with this issue. Claims by workers on Scale AI’s platforms led to a proposed settlement that has received preliminary approval in San Francisco. The company denies wrongdoing. A separate class action suit alleges Mercor misclassified more than 30,000 lawyers, doctors, and engineers while setting their pay and reviewing their work. Mercor’s clients have included OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, but they are not named as defendants. The case is ongoing.

A cardiologist who is offered a three-digit hourly rate is not in the same position as Rogelio and his family. What connects them is the fact that the rate can change suddenly, the task can disappear without warning, and they have no say over any of it.

The work will keep moving, and the rules will continue to be set by the companies that commission it, wherever the next worker logs on, whether in Kenya, Venezuela, or the Philippines.
