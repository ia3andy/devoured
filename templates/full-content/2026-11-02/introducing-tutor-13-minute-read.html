Introduction

Today, we are releasing Ampersand Tutor (&Tutor), a model trained to teach, available via API.

&Tutor learns to teach by training against Persimmon, our large-scale user model, which plays the learner. &Tutor achieves state-of-the-art performance on TutorMoments [1], a benchmark built from real tutoring moments.

A core belief at humans& is that models can better understand us and optimize for our long-term goals by learning from long human interactions. Teaching brings this belief into sharp focus. It requires understanding what a learner knows [2] and building on that understanding to help them learn [3]. Our evaluations examine how this training changes &Tutor’s teaching behavior and how those changes are driven by Persimmon.

In this blog post, we describe how we built and evaluated &Tutor. Then, we describe the evaluations we designed to validate Persimmon’s ability to model human learners. We are making &Tutor available to the public today via API.

&Tutor

A good tutor helps learners develop lasting understanding. We built &Tutor’s training environment around this principle, rewarding the tutor by how much the student learns. We train against Persimmon as a learner model. Isolating these effects, &Tutor is not trained from Ampersand, although we plan to integrate it into future Ampersand training. Our evaluations below show that this training environment encourages many downstream teaching behaviors associated with good pedagogy.

Next, we go into detail on our evaluations of &Tutor.

TutorMoments

TutorMoments [1] is built from real tutoring sessions with real students. Experienced math teachers pick out key moments where a tutor has to decide whether to offer support or push the learner to think harder, and note what each moment calls for. Models continue each conversation from that moment, with an AI model playing the student, and an LLM judge scores their responses against the teachers’ annotations. We evaluate tutor models under both a plain prompt (which just asks the model to tutor well) and an evaluation-aware prompt (which explicitly describes the balance between support and rigor).

Results. Under the plain prompt, &Tutor scores 0.754 overall on TutorMoments, compared with 0.500 for the GLM-5.3 model it was trained from. It outperforms leading frontier models, including Claude Sonnet 5.5 (0.667) and GPT-6 Astra (0.634). With the evaluation-aware prompt, which raises every model’s score, &Tutor scores 0.919, just behind GPT-6 Astra (0.926), and has the highest score of any model on appropriate rigor (0.931).

Importantly, none of these behaviors are trained for directly. &Tutor is rewarded based on how much the student learns. The pedagogical skills of knowing when to scaffold, when to push for rigor, and when to hold back all emerge implicitly from training.

Our results also suggest that the learner model plays a key role in the training environment and determines the quality of the resulting tutor model. We ran an ablation training a tutor model with the same recipe but with GLM-5.3 as a learner instead of using Persimmon. This ablation significantly underperforms &Tutor trained against Persimmon. This result is explained by the fact that, as we later show in the learner evaluations, Persimmon is a much stronger model of a learner than GLM-5.3.

The real test of a model like &Tutor is how well it helps people learn over time in real-world settings. We look forward to collaborating with teams working in education to evaluate it in those settings.

Simulated learners have long been a research topic in education [4, 5], used to give teachers practice, evaluate teaching methods, and study how students learn, and language models have recently made them far more capable [6, 7, 8, 3, 9, 10, 11, 12]. Good simulated learners can in turn be used to train tutors [13, 14, 15]. Scaling this approach depends on how well the simulated learners model real learning behavior. If they behave differently from real people, &Tutor may learn to exploit those differences, producing gains in simulation that fail to translate to real-world teaching. The quality of the learner model is therefore central to training &Tutor.

We used Persimmon as our model of learners after extensive validation of its ability to faithfully model the behavior of real human learners. We specifically show that Persimmon is better able to model the distribution of real learners’ behavior over many dimensions relevant to learning, including conversational flows, affect, and error patterns.

To evaluate how closely simulated learners resemble real people, we generate learner responses using real tutoring conversations as a starting point. We then compare the simulated and real learners across four datasets.

Two datasets capture learners working with AI tutors. These are SimulatorArena Math [16], where people work through competition math problems, and StudentBench [17], one-hour GRE tutoring sessions. For these datasets, the simulated learner continues from the real learner’s first message over multiple learner turns, interacting with the same AI tutor prompt and model, or the closest available version where the original model has been retired.

The other two, Eedi [18] and TutorMoments [1], capture learners working with human tutors. Since we can’t replay a human tutor, we generate a single learner turn at a time, using the real conversation up to that point. We repeat this at each learner turn in the original conversation. In both settings, we match the original number of learner turns.

To produce a model that can teach many kinds of human learners, we need to train it against a learner model that can reproduce the distribution of human learning behavior. For this reason, we evaluate Persimmon’s ability to match the overall distribution of real learning behavior. We report results using the multi-user Turing test methodology we introduced in our previous blog post.

For the evaluations below, we use open-coding methodology [19], automated with language models as in recent work [20], to build taxonomies of learning behavior across different dimensions.

For each dataset and dimension, we sample 3,000 real learner turns uniformly from conversations that are not in the evaluation set, and hold out every turn from 20% of those conversations. A model (Gemini 3 Flash) reads the preceding conversation and describes, in a few words, what the learner is doing along that dimension. A second model (Claude Sonnet 4.6) groups these descriptions into a small set of mutually exclusive categories. For conversational moves, these describe actions such as answering incorrectly or tentatively, requesting an explanation, proposing a strategy, or challenging the tutor.

Three independent annotator models (Gemini 3 Flash, Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Claude Haiku 4.5) then label every sampled turn, and the two-of-three majority vote is the label. Categories with fewer than 3% of the majority labels are folded into “other”, repeated until none remain. We then refine for up to three rounds: categories the annotators often confuse with each other are merged or have their definitions sharpened, stopping once agreement on the building split reaches κ = 0.72. Learners exhibit different conversational “moves” [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. The moves learners make in a conversation, such as answering, guessing, explaining their reasoning, asking for help or disengaging, reflect how they are engaging with the material and are associated with how much they learn [26].

Results. Persimmon's mix of conversational moves is the closest to real learners' on all datasets, at a distance of 0.06–0.24 from the real learners, against 0.10–0.65 for the other models.

Real learners move between confusion, frustration, engagement and boredom [27]. We open-code each learner's turn for the affect the learner expresses, build a dataset-specific taxonomy of affective states, and compare the real and simulated distributions.

Results. Persimmon also comes closest to the affect real learners express on all datasets, at a distance of 0.06–0.22, against 0.12–0.73 for the other models.

A realistic learner should be wrong about as often as real learners. We label every learner turn on all four datasets as a correct claim, an incorrect claim, or no checkable claim.

Results. Persimmon's mix of correct, incorrect and non-claim turns is the closest to real learners' on all datasets, at a distance of 0.03–0.05, against 0.16–0.33 for the other models. Real learners are wrong on 25–30% of their checkable claims, Persimmon on 19–33%, and the other models on 15% or fewer.

Conclusion

We built &Tutor by training it to help learners understand, with Persimmon playing the learner. This idea extends beyond tutoring. Helping people understand is central to good communication and to giving them more agency. Both are at the heart of the AI we want to build. We’re excited about how far this can go. As our models of people improve, so will the AI we can train to help them.
