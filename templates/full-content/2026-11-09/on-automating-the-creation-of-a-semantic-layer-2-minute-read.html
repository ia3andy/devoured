Third talk of the day at MotherDuck's Data Outpost was Sam Redfern, a staff data scientist at Canva, on automating the creation of a semantic layer. His pitch: your users can write the business context for your semantic layer, and they don’t even have to know they’re doing it.

Sam started tinkering in 2024, pasting query history into an LLM so it could write SQL against tables he didn’t know yet. That turned into an internal MCP-based data agent. Now about 90% of Canva’s data scientists use their in-house data AI tools every week, and close to 10% of the company does.

The core trick is a couple of lines in a tool description. Every time the agent runs SQL, it adds two comments: what the query is for, and any business terms or internal jargon involved. Sessions run 20 to 50 queries, often starting from a question someone pasted in from Slack, so you end up with a running record of how people actually talk about the business.

Sam’s team borrowed from Anthropic’s Clio research to mine it. They pull top topics by department, find synonyms (one team says “learning activations,” another says “first lesson”), decode the pile of overlapping three-letter acronyms, and build evals. That last one was the part I liked most. Most teams only write evals for what an agent should answer. Sam’s point is that the scarier cases are what it shouldn’t, like an agent happily ranking managers by attrition because it found an HR history table.

It has limits. The team that owns a dataset talks about it very differently from everyone else asking about it, so you need both vocabularies. And the comments usually don’t tell you what someone concluded at the end of a session, which Sam treats as a privacy upside.

His takeaways: use a data agent you can customize, build your semantic context but also put time into generating it, and if you’re paying for inference, insist on getting the full output, traces included. Then read them. Canva runs “AI Deliberate Intentional Practice” sessions where one person watches a colleague use these tools, and it turns out almost nobody is using AI the way they think they are.
