One Proton Away

Many of us grew up on Cinderella, so you probably remember the Fairy Godmother. She waves her wand and a pumpkin becomes a carriage, the mice become horses, and Cinderella’s rags become a ball gown. Then the clock hits midnight and everything turns back.

That wand is what alchemists spent centuries chasing. Mostly they wanted to turn cheap metals into gold. Without modern physics, though, all they could really do was chemistry, and the best chemistry could manage was something that looked like gold: a shiny alloy, or a thin gold coating over something cheaper.

Here is why it never worked: chemistry only moves around the outer edge of an atom, the electrons that decide how atoms stick together. It does not touch what makes gold…gold. That lives in the center of the atom, and it comes down to protons: gold has exactly 79 protons. Add one more and you have mercury (80 protons). So the difference between a gold necklace and a metal that can poison you is exactly one proton. Pulling a proton out takes roughly a million times more energy than breaking a chemical bond, which no ancient furnace or acid was ever going to deliver.

The breakthrough came from physics, once scientists learned to hit atoms with particles and neutrons. In 1980, a team led by Glenn Seaborg made a few specks of gold out of bismuth (a brittle, silvery metal), but it cost far more to produce than the new gold was worth. So it could be done, but not economically. The hard part has always been how to make it affordable. On October 21, Marathon Fusion, a San Francisco fusion startup, said it had finally made gold from mercury in a way that could one day pay for itself. Worth noting: Marathon has not published how much gold it made, and the process needs a fusion plant that does not exist yet. So this is a signal, but not an actual gold mine yet.

Gold makes for a great headline, but the bigger story is that we are learning to make and sort atoms on purpose. That matters for cancer drugs, reactor fuel, fusion plants and even deep-space batteries, all of which are waiting on scarce isotopes (different versions of the same element).

What is an isotope?

Every atom has a tiny center (nucleus) made of protons and neutrons.

  • Protons: These decide what the atom is. One proton is always hydrogen, two is always helium, and so on. It is also how the periodic table is ordered (the atomic number, usually at the top of each square).
  • Neutrons: Adding or removing these does not change the element on its own. It just makes the atom heavier or lighter, and each of those versions is an isotope.

The number in an isotope’s name is protons plus neutrons. Hydrogen-1 has one proton and no neutrons, and it makes up about 99.98% of all hydrogen. Hydrogen-2 (deuterium) has one of each and is stable but rare. Add one more neutron and you get hydrogen-3, better known as tritium, which is radioactive and barely exists in nature.

The Toolkit

Sorting: Nature mixes isotopes together, and the one you actually want is usually the rare one. Since isotopes of the same element behave almost identically, separating them is slow and expensive.

Transmuting: Hit an atom with neutrons and you get a heavier or lighter version of the same element, often an unstable one. As it settles down, a neutron can turn into a proton (or a proton into a neutron), and suddenly it is a different element. That is transmutation. Think of it as the Fairy Godmother’s pumpkin trick, done with physics.

From 1955 to 1963, Oak Ridge sorted lithium using about 24 million pounds of mercury, then shut the process down once it had enough, leaving a mercury cleanup that is still going on today. As far as anyone knows, China is the only country that still runs this process openly. The good news is that lasers, plasma centrifuges and new chemistry can now do the same job much more cleanly.

The Atoms that the Future Needs

The table below covers a handful of the isotopes that matter most: what each one is used for and how we get it today. You will spot a pattern pretty quickly. Almost all of them come from a Cold War leftover, an aging reactor or a single foreign supplier. Physics is not what makes them rare. It is that almost nobody has built a new way to make them in decades.

Isotope Who Needs It How we get it today
Actinium-225 Cancer drugs that carry it straight to tumor cells and destroy them from the inside, sparing healthy tissue Collected as thorium-229 decays; the thorium comes from Cold War uranium-233 stored at Oak Ridge
Uranium-235 (LEU, and HALEU at 5% to 20%) The fuel that keeps a nuclear fission reactor’s chain reaction going; new smaller reactors need a stronger blend called HALEU Gas centrifuges at a select few companies, with Russia a major supplier
Lithium-6 Nuclear fusion plants line their reactors with it, because each lithium-6 atom hit by a neutron turns into fusion fuel. Made at scale only in Russia and China; the US relies on a weapons stockpile from the 1960s
Helium-3 Quantum computers need it to chill their chips to just above absolute zero, the only temperature where many of them work. Collected from decaying tritium, in the US at Savannah River (South Carolina)
Plutonium-238 Its steady heat powers spacecraft too far from the sun for solar panels, like the Mars rovers. Neutrons hitting neptunium-237 targets at two DOE reactors in Tennessee and Idaho.

What does a world of abundant isotopes look like?

Medicine: Imagine cancer treatment without a waiting list. In 2023, a shortage of the prostate cancer drug Pluvicto left new patients waiting months to start treatment. Drugs built on even scarcer isotopes, like actinium-225, are rationed today. With enough supply, they could move out of trials and into everyday care.

Nuclear power: Most fission reactors run on uranium enriched to 3% to 5%. Many of the newer, smaller designs need a stronger blend, between 5% and 20%, so they can run longer between refuelings, and Russia is the main commercial seller. Lithium-7, which reactors use to manage their water chemistry, is the same story: it only comes from China and Russia. If supply opened up, reactor builders could stop worrying about where their fuel comes from.

Fusion: Most fusion plants plan to run on tritium, the form of hydrogen that barely exists in nature. The idea is to make it on-site by wrapping the reactor in lithium-6, which turns into tritium when a neutron hits it. Right now, nobody in the West can enrich lithium-6 at industrial scale. Solve that, and the same neutrons can also make medical isotopes, and even gold.

Quantum: Quantum computers have to be kept colder than outer space, and the fridges that get them there use helium-3. A big one holds about 40 liters, roughly $100,000 worth. Nearly all of it comes from tritium slowly decaying inside nuclear weapons stockpiles, and nobody is making more at scale. Cheap helium-3 would remove one of the stranger bottlenecks in building bigger machines.

Space and defense: Far enough from the sun, solar panels stop being useful. Spacecraft out there run on plutonium-238, which gives off steady heat for decades that a nuclear battery turns into power. The US makes only a few kilograms a year, enough for a nuclear-powered NASA mission every few years.

Where We Would Bet

Isotopes of the same element are nearly identical, which is what makes separating them so hard, and new companies are getting clever about telling them apart. Every isotope absorbs a very specific color of light, so a laser tuned to exactly the right color can give one isotope an electric charge while leaving the others alone. Then the charged atoms can be pulled out. Plasma centrifuges turn material into a charged gas and spin it so fast that the slightly heavier isotopes drift to the outside. Whoever cracks cheap sorting of isotopes (that would allow us to extract them at will) will do extremely well.

Fusion plants selling electricity to the grid are still years away because no one has sorted out how to create an economically viable fusion reactor (yet). But you do not need a full power plant to make neutrons, and neutrons are what turn ordinary atoms into valuable isotopes. SHINE already uses small fusion reactions to make medical isotopes it sells today, and Marathon plans to do the same long before its gold matters. That is real revenue for a fusion company years before it might sell its first kilowatt-hour to the grid.

Takeaway: Cancer drugs, reactor fuel, fusion plants, quantum computers and deep-space batteries all depend on a handful of isotopes from aging reactors, Cold War stockpiles or a single foreign supplier. Whoever makes sorting and transmuting atoms cheap opens up all of those markets at once, from cancer treatment without a waiting list to space vehicles that reach the edge of the solar system.

And this time, the magic does not wear off at midnight.
