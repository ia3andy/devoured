• At Meta, we’ve built a system that enhances ads recommendations by leveraging idle inference capacity in our fleet.
  • Two elasticity levers in the system allow ads recommendation work to expand across opportunistic capacity, without compromising baseline performance.
  • This post describes how we designed and now operate the system at Meta’s scale and how it drives significant advertiser value and capacity efficiency.

Inference fleets face a capacity paradox: They can be globally constrained while meaningful pockets of capacity remain locally idle. At Meta, we built a system that aggregates these pockets into a shared opportunistic capacity pool and turns them into better ads recommendations. By putting otherwise idle capacity to work, the system helps people see more relevant ads and improves advertiser outcomes without requiring additional capacity orders.

Using this opportunistic capacity safely is difficult. It’s distributed across team-owned capacity allocations, regions, and accelerator types, and may be reclaimed with little notice. The ads inference system must therefore adapt workload as capacity supply changes without overloading the dedicated capacity that serves baseline workload.

We address this challenge with two complementary elasticity levers. Rank More Ads (RMA) adjusts the number of candidates evaluated by the ranking stages. Serve Better Models (SBM) adjusts the share of requests routed to a more powerful model.

Physical and logical isolation protects the capacity reserved for baseline serving. Separate feedback loops control RMA candidate volume and SBM model coverage, while value-aware load shedding at both servers and clients allows the system to degrade gracefully when capacity is suddenly reclaimed. Together, these mechanisms allow optional work to expand and contract with opportunistic capacity while protecting latency and reliability.

The Inference Capacity Paradox: Globally Constrained, but Locally Idle

Inference fleets must maintain enough capacity to meet demand under strict latency and reliability targets. However, demand and capacity rarely align perfectly across time, regions, resource combinations, and accelerator types. Capacity can therefore remain idle in parts of a fleet even when the fleet is constrained overall. Common causes include:

  • Peak-provisioning and demand-variation mismatch: Inference fleets must be provisioned for peak demand, while traffic varies by time of day and season. Capacity needed during peak periods can therefore remain temporarily idle when demand is lower.
  • Model-development and capacity-delivery timing mismatch: Inference capacity arrives on supply-chain timelines, whereas models are developed and launched on separate schedules. This creates a gap between when capacity becomes available and when models are ready to consume it.
  • Regional resource-ratio mismatch: Inference workloads require accelerators as well as supporting resources such as CPUs. When these resources are not available in the required proportions, one resource can remain idle while another limits serving capacity.
  • Accelerator-type mismatch: Inference fleets can span multiple accelerator vendors, generations, and architectures. Workloads are not always portable across these accelerator types, which can strand capacity on hardware that cannot immediately serve the available demand.

These mismatches create an industry-wide inference capacity paradox: A fleet can be globally constrained while meaningful pockets of capacity remain locally idle. At Meta’s scale, these temporary pockets can collectively represent substantial capacity and create better ads recommendations when used safely.

From Fragmented Reservations to a Shared Opportunistic Capacity Pool

Across a heterogeneous fleet at Meta’s scale, instantaneous idle inference capacity is inevitable — even with proactive planning designed to minimize structural mismatches. However, because this capacity is inherently tied to fluctuating baseline demands, it comes with distinct operational constraints — it’s preemptible (can be reclaimed at any time), highly volatile in its availability, and carries no long-term guarantees.

To successfully consume this volatile resource without compromising baseline performance, workloads must exhibit certain structural properties:

  • A high degree of workload elasticity: The workload can quickly add or remove a large amount of optional work as the pool changes without affecting baseline expectations.
  • Sustained quality at scale: The workload can continue to meaningfully improve output quality across the range the opportunistic pool can supply. This type of workload often already uses a large amount of dedicated capacity due to this property.
  • Limited cross-service dependencies: A self-contained workload reduces the number of upstream and downstream systems that must respond to volatile capacity.

Two Complementary Elasticity Levers

Based on these structural properties, we identified two complementary levers that meet these criteria to consume this idle capacity and generate large value upside.

Rank More Ads (RMA)

For each user request, the ads recommendation system must select a small number of ads from millions of candidates in less than a second. The system uses a sequence of ranking stages in which increasingly powerful machine learning models evaluate progressively smaller candidate sets. The later stages only evaluate the top candidates from the previous stage.

RMA increases the number of candidates evaluated by the ranking stages when opportunistic capacity is available. Evaluating more candidates increases the chance of finding ads that are relevant to the user. Because the additional candidates pass through CPU-heavy upstream (e.g., retrieval, feature processing) as well as accelerator-heavy inference, RMA generally requires both CPU and accelerator surplus.

Serve Better Models (SBM)

The ads evaluation can use models with different levels of complexity. More powerful models can improve prediction quality, but it’s not efficient to use them for every request with dedicated capacity.

SBM changes the share of requests evaluated by the more powerful models as accelerator capacity changes. It keeps the number of ads entering the ranking stages constant, so upstream CPU demand remains comparatively stable. This allows SBM to use accelerator-only surplus that RMA cannot consume.

The SBM model-router component uses request-value signals to prioritize the requests expected to benefit most from a more powerful model.

Why Both Levers Are Needed

The two levers consume different combinations of resources. RMA requires both CPU and accelerator capacity, while SBM can use accelerator capacity without substantially increasing upstream CPU work. Together, they can use a broader range of opportunistic capacity than either lever alone.

The incremental impact of each lever also changes with traffic, current coverage, and the available resource mix. At a given moment, the next unit of opportunistic capacity may surface more relevant ads by ranking additional candidates to serve better ads or by routing more requests to a powerful model for improved prediction accuracy. We periodically adjust the allocation between RMA and SBM based on their measured performance and resource requirements.

Safely Using Opportunistic Capacity for Ads Inference

Opportunistic capacity can be reclaimed faster than the control loops that regulate work across a large serving fleet. We therefore design the system around one requirement – losing opportunistic capacity may reduce incremental ranking work, but it must not overload the capacity that serves the baseline workload. We support this requirement through isolated routing targets, two workload-specific feedback loops, and fast local load shedding.

Protecting Baseline Traffic Through Physical and Logical Isolation

Optional work must not consume the capacity reserved for baseline serving. SBM and RMA enforce this separation differently.

For SBM, the isolation is physical. The baseline and more powerful models use separate routing targets. Dedicated replicas are sized to serve requests with the baseline model, while replicas on opportunistic capacity serve requests selected for the more powerful model. When opportunistic capacity contracts, the system reduces the share of requests routed to the more powerful model. Those requests use the baseline model instead, whose capacity is already provisioned for them.

For RMA, the isolation is logical. The system calculates how much additional candidate-ranking work can be supported by the model replicas deployed on opportunistic capacity and uses total capacity to set a global budget for RMA. Each model replica may receive a mixture of baseline and additional work; individual requests do not need to be physically separated. Baseline traffic is protected by limiting the total incremental work to the amount that these replicas can support.

When opportunistic capacity contracts, RMA reduces its global candidate-volume target and SBM reduces its more-powerful-model coverage. In both cases, the system removes optional work rather than allowing it to spill onto the capacity reserved for baseline serving. This keeps baseline traffic within its latency and reliability targets.

Controlling Candidate Volume With Latency Feedback

RMA uses a closed feedback loop composed of a central proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controller and client-side enforcement. Looking through a simplified lens, the controller uses observed tail latency to calculate a target for the number of ads sent to downstream ranking, and clients apply that target to new requests. When latency is below the target, the controller increases the number of ads ranked. When latency rises, it reduces the number. This allows RMA to use the latency signal, which is influenced by the capacity situation, to converge on a sustainable candidate volume as conditions change.

Routing Requests With Value-Aware Model Selection

SBM uses a corresponding feedback loop with central control and client-side routing. The central controller sets the target coverage for the more powerful model, and the client-side model router decides which eligible requests use that model and which continue through the baseline model.

The router uses a request-value signal to group requests into segments based on predicted likelihood of user engagement with the ad. An explore-and-exploit process periodically measures the incremental outcome of using the more powerful model in each segment and updates the allocation between the two models. This is necessary because the incremental benefit of the more powerful model does not always align with the absolute value of a request. The process also preserves exploratory traffic across segments so the system continues to collect the observations needed to adapt, rather than reinforcing an outdated routing decision.

A calibration loop keeps the realized traffic share close to its target as the traffic mix changes across regions and over time. When capacity contracts, the central controller reduces the target coverage and the client routes fewer requests to the more powerful model. Requests removed from the opportunistic path continue through the baseline model.

Multi-Layer Value-Aware Load Shedding

The RMA and SBM feedback loops continuously align opportunistic work with available capacity, but their central components operate on the order of minutes. A sudden preemption can therefore remove capacity before clients receive new targets. The system adds value-aware protection for both workloads that can react immediately:

Server-side shedding: If the opportunistic pool loses capacity before the clients’ view has caught up, servers immediately remove lower-value opportunistic work. For RMA, they reduce the number of candidates processed within individual requests; SBM applies an equivalent value-aware shedding policy to requests routed to the more powerful model. This absorbs rapid changes close to the serving bottleneck.

Client-side shedding: The RMA and SBM clients use the latest controller targets as the guideline but also observe the server status directly. When it’s observed that the server is overloaded, clients shed lower-value opportunistic work directly, i.e., RMA requests fewer additional ads, while SBM routes fewer requests to the more powerful model.

Both forms of load shedding react within a few milliseconds, bridging the interval before the controller-client loops converge. Both server-side shedding and client-side shedding use value signals when selecting work to remove, which minimizes the effect during the transition.

Current Work and What Comes Next

Increasing Workload Elasticity

We aim to use more opportunistic capacity productively by extending the operating range of RMA and SBM and broadening the set of elasticity levers. For RMA, this means supporting higher candidate volumes across ranking stages; for SBM, using more powerful models that turn additional accelerator capacity into better predictions. We are also developing new levers such as precomputation elasticity, which performs work ahead of request time and is therefore less constrained by the location and timing of live traffic.

Expanding the Usable Capacity Pool

Opportunistic capacity is fragmented across regions and accelerator types, and it’s useful only when compatible models and supporting resources are available in the same place. We are expanding model portability and cross-region inference capabilities so that more of the available capacity can be used efficiently.

Automating Value-Based Capacity Allocation

The incremental impact of each lever changes with traffic, coverage, and the available resource mix. At a given moment, the next unit of opportunistic capacity may surface more relevant ads by ranking additional candidates or by routing more requests to a powerful model. We are building an optimization-based system to automatically adjust the allocation of AI inference capacity to its possible elasticity levers based on the benefits to users and advertisers. The goal is to maximize the return of the capacity subject to the constraints that each use case has in terms of regional constraints, hardware compatibility, and durability requirements for the idle capacity, among others.
