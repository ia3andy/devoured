Pushing the US Open-Weight Frontier

Since we launched Harvey Tenet in August, there has been a surge of progress in open-weight models, including from US labs at Meta, NVIDIA, Reflection, and Thinking Machines. In this work, we set out to understand how the latest models perform on agentic knowledge work and how much post-training improves that performance.

With Baseten, we post-trained four recently released US open-weight models – Muse Spark Open, Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, Beam 1.0, and Inkling 2 Small – using a recipe adapted from our Harvey Tenet work. For comparison, we also post-trained GLM-5.3, a non-US open-weight model of similar size. We evaluated each model on agent benchmarks spanning law, consulting, banking, business automation, and software engineering.

Our results show that post-training leads to substantial gains in both agentic legal reasoning and task completion across the models we studied. Specifically, we find:

  1. Muse Spark Open achieves 30.3% all-pass on LAB after post-training, the highest all-pass rate reported on the benchmark thus far and the first model to surpass 30% overall.
  2. Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, Beam 1.0, and Inkling 2 Small each score above 20% all-pass on LAB after post-training, surpassing the mark we reached previously with Kimi K3, a model more than three times their size.
  3. Performance does not regress in other capability areas. The post-trained models maintain their scores on non-legal benchmarks and even show some improvement on SWE-bench Pro beyond what is gained purely through increased reasoning. We plan to study this behavior more in future work.

Performance

We post-trained each model with an extension of our Harvey Tenet recipe to improve its ability to perform long-horizon, agentic legal tasks. First, we find that post-training consistently leads to strong in-distribution gains in agentic legal reasoning. Muse Spark Open successfully completes 58% more tasks on Legal Agent Bench and is the first model to break 30% all-pass on LAB. Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, Beam 1.0 and Inkling 2 Small also each exceed 20% all-pass. These results suggest that US open-weight models are becoming competitive options for agentic legal work alongside the non-US models we have previously studied.

Next, we find these improvements generalize to other legal agent benchmarks, including LAB Contracts, Mercor’s APEX Agents (Corporate Law), and Crosby’s Redline Bench, where each post-trained model scores higher than its base. Inkling 2 Small completes more than three times as many LAB Contracts tasks, increasing all-pass from 4.0% to 13.0%. Beam 1.0’s Redline Bench score increases from 40.6% to 51.1%. None of these benchmark tasks were used in our post-training, showing that the improvements carry across legal tasks and agent harnesses.

We also evaluated the models on AutomationBench, GDPval, APEX Agents (Consulting and Banking), and SWE-bench Pro (HARD-51) to check for regressions outside of the legal domain. The models generally maintain performance on these benchmarks and, surprisingly, improve on SWE-Bench Pro despite no software engineering tasks being included in training.

Lawyer preference

We ran a pairwise generative reward model (GRM) tournament, comparing work products from both original and post-trained models on held-out LAB tasks. For each task, the grader compared the two deliverables in both presentation orders, with model identities withheld. It assessed model outputs across preference axes such as accuracy, style, tone, reasoning, and alignment.

We find substantial gains in GRM preference for Nemotron 3.5 Super VL, Beam 1.0 and GLM-5.3. In direct comparisons, the grader prefers each post-trained model over its respective base in 98% of judgments. For Muse Spark Open and Inkling 2 Small, neither head-to-head win rate differs significantly from 50%, despite both models improving on LAB rubrics.

These results suggest that optimizing rubrics alone does not consistently improve preference for the final work product. The three models that improve in preference start below 5% all-pass on LAB, while Muse and Inkling start above 10% and improve task completion without a corresponding preference gain. One possible explanation is that rubric rewards improve work-product quality by correcting failures in task execution, but offer less guidance on improving an already capable model’s writing.

Efficiency

On LAB, post-trained Muse Spark Open outperformed the original model at every tested reasoning setting. It reached 30.3% all-pass rate, compared with 25.8% for the original model at maximum effort, while generating 36% fewer output tokens per task. These results suggest that post-training improves legal-task performance beyond the gains observed from increasing reasoning effort alone.

Training

We post-trained each model's released checkpoint via asynchronous reinforcement learning (RL) in LAB-style agent environments. During training, the agent worked through long-horizon legal tasks and was graded on the work product it delivered via expert rubrics.

Training reward and hold-out performance improved together early in training. Among the checkpoints evaluated, we selected the checkpoint after 80 optimizer steps for four models and after 200 steps for Beam 1.0.

Environments

Each task placed an agent in a closed-universe client matter with a short instruction requesting work. The client matter environment included both key and peripheral documents, requiring the agent to identify relevant information across files. It worked in a sandboxed workspace with tools to search the matter, read documents, and draft work product.

Reward

An LLM judge marked each rubric criterion pass or fail. Our reward was a weighted sum of the fraction of criteria passed, the fraction of legal issue groups passed in full, and a bonus for passing every criterion.

Optimization

We trained a rank-64 LoRA adapter for each model with CISPO, using the advantage and loss normalization of Dr. GRPO. For each task, we sampled a group of rollouts and computed each rollout’s advantage as its reward minus the group mean.

Conclusion

Our results show that a post-training recipe developed for agentic legal work can be adapted across model families to improve legal task completion. This enables us to build on the advances of open weight models as their capabilities improve while continuing to refine our own training methods for legal work.

Appendix

Models

Muse Spark Open (Meta): A sparse MoE Transformer with 816B total parameters and 42B active per token. It uses perception encoders for multimodal inputs.

Nemotron 3.5 Super VL (NVIDIA): The smallest model in the study, with 120B total parameters and 12B active per token. It combines Transformer attention with Mamba-2 state-space layers.

Beam 1.0 (Reflection): A sparse MoE model with 501B total parameters and 23B active per token. It combines gated grouped-query attention with sliding-window attention.

Inkling 2 Small (Thinking Machines Lab): Has 594B total parameters and 17B active per token. Its 48-layer architecture uses local and global attention.

GLM-5.3 (Z.ai): A sparse MoE model with 744B total parameters and 40B active per token. It uses DeepSeek Sparse Attention.
