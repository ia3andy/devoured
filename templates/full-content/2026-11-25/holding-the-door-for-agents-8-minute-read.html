The agents, man. They’re everywhere.

At least 55% of deployments to Fly.io are now performed by agents. Bots passed humans in web traffic for the first time this year, mostly driven by the surge in agent use. Just a year ago, Cursor had 2.5x as many autocomplete users as they did agent users. In February, they reported that the ratio had flipped, with agent users now outnumbering autocomplete by 2x. We’re watching agent use transform our customer base in real time.

This isn’t a “trend.” Agents aren’t going to dramatically wane in popularity in a few years like sourdough starters. This is the new frontier, and companies are going to live or die based on how friendly they are to the idea that humans are increasingly one step removed from making product decisions.

We have to keep up here, for two reasons. One is that we have to stay in step with the market (we are, despite all this whimsical bird stuff, a business).

But the more important reason is that we just want to help you ship cool stuff; we try pretty hard not to have preconceived notions about how you do that, because being prescriptive is bossy and uncool. With my CEO hat on, I call it “meeting developers where they are.” So if we’re paying attention to the data, it’s telling us that (many) users want to work through agents completely, and that they do not want to be pulled out of a flow state to go be a human body and click some buttons.

Roger that. So, we shipped a couple things to try and meet that need.

Making the front door agent-sized

Agents can sign up for a Fly.io account now

Filling out sign-up forms is for people with time to burn. We now support auth.md, an open spec for agent signup. It’s a simple thing really, just a Markdown file that tells agents how to register. Ours is at fly.io/auth.md.

  1. Your agent asks you which email address to use, then registers with it.
  2. It sends you a link and a code. You open the link, confirm the address with GitHub, Google or an emailed link, and type the code. If you don’t have an account yet, you name it and it’s created right there.
  3. Your agent collects its own credentials for Fly.io and Sprites, and goes back to work.

The agent’s credentials are only valid for 24 hours, and it can renew them itself. They cover the apps, Machines, volumes, secrets, deploys and Sprites in your personal organization, but not your org’s members or billing. It shows up on your tokens page as “Agent (auth.md)”, and revoking any one of its tokens cuts off its Fly.io access immediately (Sprites access ends within 24 hours).

Agents can pay

When all you want is for your agent to get a job done, it’s really annoying when it brings its whole operation to a halt just to ask you to log in somewhere and pay $1 for something. Now there are two ways your agent can pay for things it needs, safely and under suitable human supervision.

Fly.io account top up

Firstly, your agent can top up your Fly.io account now. When your org needs more credit, your agent can buy it over the Machine Payments Protocol (MPP) without anyone opening the dashboard:

  1. The agent asks the credits endpoint for, say, $25 of credit for your org.
  2. Fly.io answers 402 Payment Required with a payment challenge for that amount. HTTP has kept that status code “reserved for future use” since the 90’s. Well, the future is here and it’s an agent with a wallet.
  3. The agent pays the challenge with Stripe’s link-cli, which turns it into a spend request. You get a notification in the Link app and approve it.
  4. Fly.io adds the credit to your balance, and it works exactly like credit bought from the dashboard.

The agent also has to say why it’s spending: the request carries a human language reason that you really should read before you approve. Amounts are fixed at $5, $10, $25, $50 or $100, and the agent authenticates with a Fly.io token that can be made to expire in an hour.

Spend from a Sprite

For everything that isn’t Fly.io, your agent can reach for the Link connector. A domain, an API plan, whatever you want your agent to just buy instead of ask for. It’s easy to use. Your agent in a Sprite asks for a specific amount to spend at a specific merchant and says why. You approve it in the Link app, and your agent gets a single-use virtual card capped at the approved amount. That card stops working after 12 hours. The Sprites gateway lets through only the calls needed to request and collect a payment, so the agent can’t approve its own requests.

A unified MCP server and agent-friendly docs

One MCP server gives your agent Sprites, your apps, Machines, volumes, secrets and Managed Postgres. You decide what it can touch in a browser, with no token to paste. Also, the whole of fly.io is indexed and optimized specifically for agents, so they can read the docs themselves (let them brag about how much faster they are at it, it’s all they have).

The Sprites ecosystem is all grown up

What started as a few Sprites Connectors for the most popular agents has become a serious toolkit for agents working in Sprites. Give Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, OpenCode, Hermes and more a real computer that you don’t have to be precious about. There are SDKs for 8 languages and frameworks and 61 connectors, which let a Sprite call the services you want your agent to use, securely. Whatever your agent runs on, and whichever toys it likes to play with, they are probably on the list.

We’re still a tool for devs

So we’re building all these agent doorways, trying to make it as easy as possible for people who want to realize the dream of never interacting with a UI again. (You’re missing out on a lot of cool purple buttons, but hey, it’s your journey.) Which: fun. Obviously we like building new whizbang stuff.

We’re also wondering (like everyone else) what it means for The Industry that we’re paving the way for agents to run so much of the research → analysis → selection → payment → deployment loop. What will happen as a result of everything going through an agentic bouncer before it reaches the devs we’re used to building for?

Two ideas

First of all: who knows, we’re no more clairvoyant than you are.

But we are, as a group, pretty optimistic, and we’re seeing some positive stuff: a closed agentic loop that lets people on either end of the technical capability spectrum effectively gain super powers.

We’re getting pretty close to people with zero technical background being able to build with actual autonomy. There’s been a lot of talk about this in the past year, but if you actually watched a non-engineer friend try to build their own product, there’s always a moment when the agent has to break the fourth wall and ask for an API key or something, and the whole thing grinds to a halt. That’s pretty swiftly becoming a thing of the past; if you can keep the entire process in natural language, you’re going to have people who can build cool things with no idea how it is getting done. We like this. We are pro people having competencies in other areas (like…healthcare, maybe? Or clean energy?) and not having to go through someone else in order to make their thing exist.

And obviously, experienced engineers are now able to punch orders of magnitude above their weight class. We had a call with a customer last week, a speed-scaling insurance agency for wealthy households, working on redefining what that looks like in an agentic world. Engineering team? Two people.

It’s the same story in our own workshops and pretty much every agentic tool we build for ourselves is happening in Sprites these days. Why wouldn’t we reach for a hardware-isolated, purpose-built computer for agents?

One of our favourite projects is our software factory. You give it a job (“fix this bug”, “tell me what this error means”) from its web UI, a Slack mention, or an alert from Sentry, and it sends the work down a line of agents. One researches the request and finds the cause, one makes the change, one uses the running product to confirm the change worked, one reviews it independently.

Each agent is just Claude Code on its own Sprite. The work order lives in code, so an agent can’t talk its way past a gate, and the whole flow ends in a draft pull request. Obviously the agents can’t merge or touch anything related to authentication, billing or production without a human thumbs-up.

The cool part (if you ask us) is the second loop. When a job finishes, a separate agent called the Scorer reads back through every step and grades the run against five criteria that live in a markdown file. The same things you’d nag any engineer about. Is every claim in the report backed by a command it actually ran? Would the new tests fail without the change? Did it do what was asked and nothing else? Did it burn steps rerunning the same failing command? Those pile up, and when the same criterion fails three times in two weeks, another agent goes looking for the cause and opens a draft pull request against the agents’ own prompts. That pull request goes down the same line as any other change, and a human decides whether it merges. Self iteration FTW.

Okay, Fine, Everybody In

Not to be cavalier/kumbaya about the whole thing, but our real job here is the same as it always has been: get out of the way and make it easy for you to build. It looks like that now means giving agents whatever they need in order to act as proxies while you spin a bunch of plates at once. Great! Climb on in, agents. There’s enough hot air balloon for everyone.
