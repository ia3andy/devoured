Consumption pricing makes sense on paper: you pay for what you use. The trouble starts when a team can’t see what it’s using, can’t estimate what next month looks like, or can’t do anything about high usage until it’s too late.

When that happens, people ration. They hold back a new dashboard or alert because they aren’t sure what it will cost. They dispute bills and hesitate to adopt tools they are paying for. That hesitation is costly because it slows teams down in the places observability is meant to speed them up.

Picture a platform engineer about to create an alert on a service’s error rate. New Relic shows an estimate of what it will cost before they save it, but a number on its own doesn’t say much. Is that high or low for their team? Without a view of usage and a budget to compare it against, the alert can sit while someone works that out. With both in place, the engineer can judge the number, decide with confidence and get the alert live while it’s still useful.

Planning for rising spend

In New Relic’s 2026 Observability Forecast, 72% of the 2,575 IT and engineering leaders and practitioners surveyed expect their observability spend to rise over the next 12 months, and AI adoption is the top driver for the second year running.

Most organizations expect spend to grow, and that’s fine as long as they can see it coming.

The aim is to make cost visible, estimable, and controllable, so it never stops a team from using or growing on the platform. This is where governance comes into play.

What we mean by governance

Governance isn’t a gate or committee. It’s a set of guardrails you put in place before spend happens, so the people closest to the work can move quickly inside them.

New Relic has two meters to govern. Data is what you ingest, measured in gigabytes (GB). Compute is the work New Relic does on your data, such as queries, dashboards, and alert conditions, measured in Compute Capacity Units (CCU). Much of it runs in the background. Each has its own levers and we’ll cover them in this series.

Four stages as a continuous loop

We treat governance as a loop you run continuously.

  1. Set Up: Build the foundation before you spend: an account structure that mirrors your teams, a named budget owner, scoped access, and a decision about which paid capabilities are on, and for whom. Is our account built so we can see and control cost by the team that drives it?
  2. Control: Set guardrails against your plan: budgets, alerts, and user limits that notify, or block, before the bill does. When we approach the plan, do the right people get told, or does the spend get stopped, in time?
  3. Track: Watch usage against those guardrails, by account, capability, and user. If usage moves, how fast can we see it and find the cause?
  4. Optimize: Reduce waste while keeping the signal you depend on. Tune the alerts and dashboards that burn the most compute, and filter noisy data before you store it. Are our most expensive queries, alerts, and logs also our most valuable?

Where are you today?

Use this quick test: when your last bill arrived, was any of it a surprise? If the invoice is the first place you find out something changed, you're early in the loop, and the next move is simple: name an owner and set a budget. If you already get alerts but mostly react after a spike, add limits and thresholds against your plan. Each level has a next step.
