Forward-Deployed Design As Plumbing

Notes on forward-deployed design, coordination debt and two classroom builds of scoped public fixes

I’m revisiting the forward-deployed design discussion, and I think the shape of teams is less important to me. It’s also fairly apparent that this category of work isn’t taking off.

Instead, we’re getting more hybrid roles where product managers get to justify their existence by using agents to augment the work of designers. That’s a crude assessment of the situation and won’t reflect your experience if you’re in a larger-headcount megacorp or working for a consumer services company where you can’t afford not to have designers. The latter scenario works fairly well for incumbents but really badly for emerging designers looking for the ladder upward into roles, because AI is really decimating the career progressions that designers once relied on.

Graphic designers shape-shifted into information architects and user experience designers, and UXers pivoted into management or product design roles. The product designer was closer to the designer-engineer archetype that’s always existed in some orgs, but AI is upending this and making everyone feel some need to rebrand into some kind of engineer to stay semirelevant in these complicated times.

So where does that leave our nascent belief in a forward-deployed posture that implores us to go “toward” the work? The whole “Design as Repair” ethos was really about what I told friends: designers as the folks who get into coveralls and tackle the problem. I want designers to be seen as indispensable problem solvers who fix the problem once and don’t have to come back to that problem. Less engineering and more plumbing. Less weeds, more pipes.

I’ve listened to a lot of podcasts lately from AI-native design leaders who are talking about design as systems thinking and how making designers obsolete is OK, because we can replace design with “taste,” “craft” and “systems thinking” as proxies for the role of the designer. I think that’s all fine if you work for a consumer-based tool and are trying to really invest in zero-to-one imagination. Your tool might be solving some kind of consequential problem, but it’s not essential to the world in the same way that so many other problems are.

We don’t need social media or SaaS tools; we spent generations operating OK without them. What we do have is a lot of coordination debt, and technology is increasing the complexity of these problems because systems don’t integrate. IT modernization debt is focused more on hardware, tooling and an unwillingness to abandon waterfall. Your garden-variety IT department isn’t operating like a tech startup, nor should it, because if a hospital takes on risky software that doesn’t work, it could cost lives.

My time in government revealed so many broken interfaces that could be fixed but came with legacy issues. You’d be astounded by how much time and effort it takes to convince people who aren’t technical that systems need to be migrated, because they’re comfortable with tools they’ve been using and don’t understand how much things have improved and how it might help their customers. The incentives are all wrong.

So, circling back to my whole argument about where we are with design, I think we need more designers to care about things that matter, but we also need opportunity spaces to actually deploy that care. One thing I miss from the ZIRP era was how much easier it was to get money activated to launch pilots. I’m down on “civic tech” these days because I think we need a new model of tackling local problems, but the ugly problems of deployment simply aren’t getting the sort of resources they need.

My pilot of the Portland Digital Corps last year wasn’t a “design-led” social entrepreneurship venture, because I didn’t approach it that way. It was a bootstrapped response to the DOGE frame that we need to dismantle things rather than fix them. It was successful in its scope but fundamentally unsustainable, because it was self-funded and the message was hard to get out into the world.

My problem is that it’s impossible right now for me to care about the things people are talking about. I’m not “excited” about new AI models; I don’t care about the tech. I read the papers every day, local and global. What I read about are technical challenges (backlogs, waitlists, aging systems) and a lot of people working hard and yet firmly resolved to operate the way they always have, because the incentives are not there to do anything bold. The money isn’t there, and speed can be a problem for budgets when you’re an org whose only job is to tackle a mission. If someone comes along and says, “I think we can make your mission obsolete. It’ll help a lot of people, but if we’re right, you’ll need to find a new mission or shut down,” that’s less of a benefit and more of a threat.

What does this have to do with forward-deployed design? Repair is the North Star. We need to stop seeing modernization as a project and invest as if we’re fixing scoped problems that have end dates. We can’t modernize an entire agency overnight, but we can impact systems. Pilots are cheaper than they’ve ever been, and I’m not talking about slop AI user interfaces. Getting working A/B tests into the hands of normal people to see where the friction is and where the most important parts of the problem are, and being able to scope the work, has never been easier.

The best part for a designer is that we can do this ourselves with the right mandates, but finding the space to do this kind of speculative R&D work that you can actually implement is more challenging than just launching things. The price to reverse-engineer problems has never been lower. If we’d had the ability to hand someone a pilot at 18F without needing lengthy MOUs, and a chance to help them visualize the shape of their problems more clearly, it would have been a game changer. The idea that we’re just going to deploy a bunch of expert teams to inject AI into systems without reasoning about what we’re trying to solve is a bad gambit. We know what the problems are. Open a newspaper website and read about them, locally and beyond. Bureaucracy crops up in a ton of places; you don’t have to look far.

In my Public Mechanics (URP610/UT402) class at the University of Michigan’s Taubman College of Architecture and Urban Planning, we spent a few weeks talking about governance, mandates, systems and tooling.

East Detroit

East Detroit is a resident portal for a fictional consolidated city and county, built on the X-Road idea that every agency keeps its own records while the portal pulls in only what a service needs. When Samira Ortiz has a baby, the state’s birth record adds her daughter to the household and extends her health coverage. WIC and the library each offer a service she can accept, and she never fills out a form.

The East Detroit Card gets residents into the library, rec centers and transit, and each resident chooses what every one of those places is allowed to learn about them. Anyone can see what their household may qualify for without signing in.

In class, students played the city departments and outside agencies, deciding which arrangements to accept, limit or reject, while I pitched as the vendor and then pushed as the mayor.

You can see the East Detroit demo here.

Snaprates

Snaprates is a working model of how a state could catch SNAP payment errors before benefits go out, set in Absaroka, a fictional state with 10,000 generated cases. A separate checker reads a stripped-down copy of every benefit decision and redoes the math against the pay stubs and rent records in the file.

Any mismatch lands on an eligibility worker’s list as a specific question, and the worker makes the final call on every case. The state’s existing eligibility system stays in place the whole time. In the run the demo carries, the simulated error rate fell from 10% to 5.4%. In the seminar, each student works the queue from one of four seats: eligibility worker, eligibility specialist, quality control reviewer or program administrator.

The fictional SNAP pilot is here.

What I like about both of these is that they work inside systems that already exist. Nobody has to migrate a platform or sign up for a multiyear transformation program, and you can measure when the work is done. That’s really what I mean when I say pilots are cheaper than they’ve ever been. A week’s build for a classroom shows a mechanism that a procurement cycle would probably spend three years failing to specify.

The expertise isn’t lost, just underutilized. The people are still out there; they just don’t have a home. They’re at consultancies, in classrooms and in big tech design orgs, trying to spin up shops or consulting for orgs behind the scenes, or they got pushed into early retirement. These people carry all the lessons from procurement fights, hallway diplomacy with CIOs and pilots that succeeded. What folks lack isn’t work; it’s a mandate and a home to do the work in. Folks need a way to work on problems they’ve actually lived through and a habit of debriefing so the lessons get passed on. There’s a hidden upside to not having a home, too.

A plumber who finishes the job and moves on has no reason to want the problem to stick around. I think the forward-deployed posture only really works when the person being deployed has nothing riding on the status quo. Salaried insiders start to get too attached to the problems and to feeling like part of the solution, instead of staying attached to knowing how to use a wrench.

It’s impossible for me to care about what people are talking about right now. State capacity is upstream from this conversation. Permit reform, endless reorgs and discussions about headcount are ancillary to what the agenda purports to be about: velocity. If we’re serious about using this disruptive technology for something other than making people rich, we need to start deploying toward longstanding problems and showing capability that directly impacts people’s lives in positive ways.

Read any social media site and you’ll find someone frustrated with some process or system. The thing people are less willing to say is that the problems most worth solving do not have a profit margin attached to them. No one is going to get rich fixing backlogs once. The difference is the motivation. With repair as the North Star, the goal is to show up looking for a scope that’s fixable and that we can test, so we can attempt bigger fixes.

Technologists have become too detached from deploying success in ways people can feel. Enough talk about the tech, the tools or the reasons we can’t, when it has never been easier to focus on enablement and on achieving goals that make a measurable impact.

Let’s get to work.
