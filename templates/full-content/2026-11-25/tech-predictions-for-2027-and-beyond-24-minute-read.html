Tech predictions for 2027 and beyond

Many areas are converging to power a golden era of innovation: robotics, artificial intelligence, space exploration, biotech, and more. When we direct these advances at some of the world’s hardest problems, we have an opportunity to make incredible progress. Yes, I am a techno optimist. I honestly believe that technology can play an incredibly positive role in our lives. But that doesn’t mean that I don’t see the dark side. Technology could just as easily be used for malicious acts and we need to put equal energy into dealing with bad actors and an unpredictable geopolitical landscape. As such, some of my predictions are a bit of a reality check, focused on things you should be thinking about right now. Others challenge you to imagine what’s possible in the decade to come.

Computing returns to biology

For most of computing’s history, memory and processing have lived in separate places. Data flowing between two disparate parts of a system, consuming energy, and producing heat. Meanwhile, the human brain has remained the most amazing machine in existence. Computing in memory, improving with use, and running with the efficiency of a small lightbulb. Increasingly, computer scientists are looking to the brain as a source of ideas, and neuroscience will increasingly influence how we design systems in the years to come.

A 2023 trip to Melbourne took me inside Cortical Labs, where human neurons grown on silicon were learning to complete tasks through trial and error. Watching living cells adapt their behavior to reach a goal has stayed with me ever since. Neuroscientist Anil Seth argues that the brain’s function depends on the physical material it is built on, which is living neurons. He believes the only way to seamlessly replace a biological neuron is with another biological neuron, and that consciousness is likely inseparable from the living matter that produces it. If he is right, a network of living neurons like the ones at Cortical Labs is a far more plausible candidate for how the brain works than any AI running on silicon.

The company has since tested living networks on Doom, where 200,000 neurons helped navigate corridors and respond to enemies. In a collaboration with the Honda Research Institute, its neurons learned a navigation task, and outperformed machine learning systems tuned specifically for it. Its first installations came online this summer, moving the technology beyond a laboratory demonstration and into early commercial use. Each installation is built on the CL1, a self-contained computer that keeps living human neurons alive on a silicon chip so they can learn and process information. A CL1 typically holds about 200,000 neurons, though the count can range from 10,000 to millions depending on the task, and it runs on 25 to 30 watts. A full rack of 30 CL1s draws under 1,000 watts, while next-generation GPU racks are expected to draw around 600 kilowatts. For larger problems, Cortical Labs can also connect many CL1s into a single system called HIVE, which has surpassed conventional AI on that same navigation task and is already being used to control robots.

The work at Cortical Labs is part of a growing field of biological computing, one of several disciplines tackling the brain’s efficiency from different directions. Chip designers are reproducing useful features of neural circuitry in silicon, and software engineers are creating multi-level memory systems that mimic the way humans manage what they learn from experience. Each approach studies some combination of local processing, adaptation, neural plasticity, and selective memory; all of it borrowed from the way the brain itself operates.

Chip designers working in neuromorphic computing are building processors that store and process information in the same place, the way the brain does. They build connections whose behavior changes with use, much as synapses strengthen or weaken, allowing a chip to keep learning after manufacturing. Researchers at the University of Texas at Dallas built a prototype whose connections strengthen when the neurons on either side of them fire together, a principle from neuroscience called Hebbian learning. The chip learned to recognize patterns and make predictions by adjusting its connections as each example arrived, with no separate training run, and it needed fewer training computations than conventional AI systems. At Peking University, researchers built a processor that performs calculations within memory and completed workloads hundreds of times faster than a leading processor. These designs reduce the costly traffic between memory and processing that has defined conventional computers. They can also let devices keep learning where network access is slow, expensive, or unavailable.

Software engineers working on memory for AI agents are reaching related conclusions because an agent needs to retain useful experience without letting old context overwhelm each new task. They can rank memories by importance, reinforce information that proves useful, compress older records, and remove details that no longer help. Recent experience can remain detailed for immediate use, while repeated lessons become more durable records. A separate process can periodically merge those records and use explicit rules to “forget” things that are not important. These choices let an agent improve over time without searching its entire history before every action.

Energy costs will be the primary driver for advancements in this field because current models demand more chips, more electricity, and more cooling. Systems that compute where they store information, learn from fewer examples, and discard unhelpful detail can reduce those costs. They can also bring computing to places where power and network access are limited, including rural hospitals, schools, farms, and remote sensors.

For builders, neuroscience now belongs on your reading list, particularly for anyone working on machines that must learn continuously within hard limits on energy and memory. The brain stores information, learns, and adapts more efficiently than anything we have built, and it remains the most amazing machine in existence. The more we learn from it, the better the systems we build will become. That’s why the next major breakthrough in computing will come from understanding how biology does more with less.

Every system defends itself

In 2027, adversarial testing will move into every phase of software development. Before software reaches the outside world, an organization’s own agents will have attacked it hundreds or thousands of times, and those attacks will continue for as long as the system runs.

When WannaCry spread through systems around the world in 2017, it ran on an exploit stolen from the NSA and struck 59 days after a patch was available. 59 days where every exposed system could have been fixed and wasn’t. Finding a flaw like that and turning it into an attack took expertise, weeks of work, and resources few organizations outside of intelligence agencies or nation-states could afford. These constraints forced attackers to pick their targets carefully and gave defenders time to patch (or at least make the tough call about what to triage now and what could go in the backlog). In the past year, the timescale to find a weakness and exploit it has collapsed. Agents now bring the expertise with them, run in swarms, and are becoming so cheap that the cost of running hundreds of them in parallel is negligible for an attacker.

In April, Anthropic described pointing a thousand copies of Claude Mythos Preview at OpenBSD, an operating system known for its focus on security, with each agent focused on a different file and given a prompt that amounted to “Please find a security vulnerability in this program.” Across a thousand runs, the work cost less than $20,000 and uncovered a 27-year-old flaw in the TCP stack that could crash any OpenBSD host responding over TCP. The run that found it cost less than $50. Microsoft later reported that generating proofs of concept for 182 confirmed Linux kernel crash findings took an average of 21.5 minutes and $3.61 per successful case. Most investigations still find nothing, but at these prices an attacker can run thousands of them and needs only one useful result.

When it comes to defense, most of the work thus far has happened in research labs or been carried out by specialized security teams at a handful of large organizations, often by pointing agents at existing codebases and uncovering bugs and exploits in production code. For example, Mozilla’s engineers fixed 423 security bugs across releases in April, compared with roughly 20 to 30 in a typical month in 2025. But this is all retroactive work. Fixing the things that had been missed, deprioritized, or punted and forgotten. And there is a lot to catch up on, as evidenced by the php2shell PHP incident that touched 70% of the internet. I expect that we will see more of these events in the coming months.

To address this, we need to change how we think about security. Adversarial testing will become standard practice for every engineering organization at every stage of development. And we will look to agents to scale the expertise that was the bottleneck. They will stress-test project plans, user stories, and architecture diagrams before any coding begins. They will review permissions and inspect every spec, pull request, and template produced, both as the code is being written and again before it is released. Before anything hits production, agents will have attacked copies of your entire environment along every path a malicious actor would have taken. And just like with Toyota’s Andon cord, which allows any employee to stop the line when a defect is found, they will prevent any known defect from shipping.

At Amazon, we are already seeing the early stages of this work internally, and as services we offer to customers. Take Autonomous Threat Analysis, a tool that uses one group of agents to invent attacks and another to test whether our defenses catch them. Or AWS Continuum, which looks for risks in your code and proves which are exploitable.

As tools continue to improve and the cost of running agents continues to fall, teams will set out to close every vulnerability path in a system, which was impractical, if not impossible, for a team of engineers just two years ago. We’ll see this first in new software because we can build these processes into our development plans from day one. Systems with years of accumulated code and tech debt will take longer. But agents can continue working through less urgent weaknesses after the most dangerous ones are fixed, constantly chipping away. In both cases, agents will free up security experts to concentrate on the work that requires human judgment, and our systems will get undeniably safer as a result.

This reminds me of a conversation I had with Jim Gray twenty years ago about Amazon’s engineering culture and the idea behind “You build it, you run it.” A system might take a year to build and then run for decades, and running it well is the responsibility of the team that built it for as long as customers depend on it, or until someone else takes over ownership. With AI, running a system well also means defending it against attackers who can probe it constantly at negligible cost. Automated defense will become part of operating a system in the same way monitoring and on-call already are.

No organization has the luxury of saying it is not a technology company anymore. Unpatched systems are your responsibility. Software schedules treatments in hospitals, moves money between banks, controls machinery on factory floors and in fulfillment centers, balances energy networks, and delivers public services. In 2027, agents will put adversarial testing in the hands of every organization, from small regional hospitals to the school down the street from your house. The days of choosing what to test will be a relic of the past because we will test everything, and we will trust systems because the people who built them attacked them first.

Sovereignty becomes a non-functional requirement

In 2027 and beyond, builders will design for sovereignty the way they design for security and cost. Where data lives, who holds the keys, and which model touches it, will be requirements a system proves on every deployment, so the answers already exist when an auditor asks.

A few years ago, the main concern I heard from CIOs was how to get finer-grained control over their cost. To address this, we developed the Frugal Architect framework, where cost is not only an “after-the-fact” issue, but something deeply integrated into design, architecture, and operations, up front. Of the seven laws of frugal architecture, the first one lays it out best: “Make cost a non-functional requirement.”

In the past year, the number one question that I get from CIOs around the world has been how to best deal with geopolitical tensions that may impact how and where they store their data, where they should run certain workloads, and which services to rely on. A sovereign architecture would address some of these concerns, and its guidelines would be remarkably similar to those of the Frugal Architect. But it all starts with making sovereignty a non-functional requirement that can be traded off against other requirements like cost, performance, and resilience.

AWS customers have always had full control over where their data was located and who could access that data. Early on, Amazon S3 introduced server-side encryption to handle certain regulatory requirements, which is now the default. This was quickly followed by the ability for customers to bring their own keys and have full control over policies, rotation, and deletion through AWS KMS. Most importantly, managing your own keys means the customer has full control over who has access to their data, not AWS or any other 3rd party.

With 39 regions, our customers have many options for where to place their workloads. These choices have been largely driven by issues such as latency, cost, and feature availability. Many customers are using multiple regions to achieve resilience or operate closer to where their customers are located. Several customers who do not have a local region, and whose operations are subject to data-locality rules, follow the patterns developed by our solution architects so that they can benefit from the cloud while still meeting their regulatory requirements.

Our European customers now have an additional option for where to place their most sensitive workloads: the AWS European Sovereign Cloud. This region has no critical dependencies outside EU borders and has been designed to continue its operations indefinitely. It has a dedicated governance structure in Europe, with a new parent company and three local subsidiaries incorporated in Germany. They are led by EU citizens who are obligated to abide by European laws and to act in the best interests of the AWS European Sovereign Cloud.

The Frugal Architect has taught us that almost everything is a trade-off, and that one of the main drivers for that are the requirements the business brings. Does each of your workloads require 4 nines availability? Or are 2 or 3 nines sufficient for the less crucial components? Each of these choices has consequences, whether those are in cost or engineering complexity. The same goes for sovereignty as a non-functional requirement; which workloads are subject to which regulatory requirements, what are the risks, and which options are there to choose from to meet these requirements and mitigate the risks.

Systems now change faster than any audit can follow. An AI agent can add a replica in another region or swap a model in minutes. Who controls the data, which regions are options, which models are alternatives, must be decided at design time, by engineers in collaboration with the business. And it starts with laying out what the risks are if certain requirements cannot be met.

Over-correcting carries its own risk. Frugality was always about spending deliberately. Sovereignty asks for the same discipline because isolation is not the same thing as control. Organizations that wall off every workload in the most restrictive environment they can find and apply the same rules to a lunch menu as to a patient record will pay in cost, in resilience, and in access to new capabilities.

The organizations that get this right will decide, workload by workload, how much sovereignty each one needs. For some that means a dedicated environment such as the AWS European Sovereign Cloud; for many it means customer-held keys and a residency rule enforced in the pipeline. They will keep the ability to move between regions and between models and rehearse that move the way they rehearse failover.

Collaboration between scientists and agents leads to new cures

Every cure starts with understanding a disease well enough to know which treatments to pursue. It also happens to be the slowest part of the research process, and sets the pace for everything that follows, from lab work to clinical trials. Agents can now help research teams examine evidence at a speed and scale no person or team could match. Before this decade is out, scientists working with agents will discover a cure for a disease that today’s medicine can only manage.

In August of this year, in a late-stage melanoma treatment trial, a personalized mRNA cancer vaccine used in combination with pembrolizumab reduced the risk of recurrence and distant metastasis compared with pembrolizumab alone. Machine learning selected the vaccine’s targets from the mutations in each patient’s tumor, a task whose scale makes it impractical to perform by hand. Related personalized vaccine programs are now being tested against other cancers. And a drug whose target and molecule were identified using computational systems entered Phase III trials for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

I want to be precise about the role agents will play in helping to cure diseases. AI already supports the daily work of scientists and clinicians, from image detection to machine learning algorithms that help predict patient outcomes. Agents will play a larger role in the discovery process, helping researchers identify the right target, or design a therapy around the biology of a disease or an individual patient. The outcome I am predicting is one in which a disease is cured or stopped in its tracks by people with the support of agents.

Some tools that were experimental only a few years ago are now part of everyday biological research. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized advances in protein structure prediction and computational protein design, while the Physics prize honored foundational work on neural networks. This year, the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine recognized optogenetics, which uses light to switch individual nerve cells on and off. It gives researchers a new way to map the brain by observing what changes when specific neurons are activated or silenced. Google DeepMind’s AlphaFold has now been used by more than three million researchers across 190+ countries, including over one million users in low- and middle-income countries. Together, these capabilities give scientists more precise ways to understand biological systems and test possible interventions.

These systems are also changing how research teams work. Agents help plan experiments and propose and rank thousands of possible interventions at a time, while scientists direct the work and verify each result. This work remains at an early stage, with many experiments to come and some of them certain to fail. Findings must be reproduced, and therapies must prove safe and effective in clinical trials. Even so, research and diagnosis are now happening at a pace that was unimaginable a few years ago. The results we are beginning to see give me confidence that scientists working with agents will discover a cure before the decade is out.

The mind becomes readable

Brain interfaces belonged to science fiction for decades, and until recently, every working version required surgery. The next generation will be non-invasive, translating your intents into actions. And within a decade, we will see devices that answer only to you, that do much more than command and control. They will amplify human capabilities in a way that no technology has before.

When you stop and think about how much information your brain processes, and how quickly it lets you act on that information with precision, consciously and unconsciously, it’s really quite amazing. Hundreds, if not thousands, of different things at once such as hunger, the sensation of chewing, the color and taste of food, how the meal makes you feel, being full, listening to and responding to your friends. And yet there is still so much that we don’t understand.

For as long as we’ve built machines, we have tried to emulate this relationship without really understanding it. Turning a thought into action. In every case, we’ve had to work through an interface. The keyboard turned language into keystrokes, the mouse let us point instead of type, touch let us manipulate the screen with our hands, and voice let us control computers with speech. Each one still required us to translate what we wanted into a physical action the machine could read. The next interface will move closer to the source, decoding neural signals and making a narrow, but powerful, part of our mind readable.

Science fiction writers have dreamed up versions of this for decades. William Gibson imagined people wiring their nervous systems into a global network. In the Culture novels, Iain M. Banks imagined neural lace, a mesh of microscopic filaments grown throughout the brain that let people control machines without speaking a word. The first real systems, albeit clunkier ones, followed the same invasive path: implanted sensors to let people with ALS speak, people with paralysis use their hands again, and people losing their sight to recover some of it. But few healthy people would accept brain surgery for a faster way to use a computer. A 2024 YouGov survey found only one in five would consider a brain implant, and in the United States that fell to 11%. For brain interfaces to move beyond the clinic and into everyday use, they will have to be non-invasive.

This summer, researchers at Meta and several European labs used a magnetoencephalography scanner outside the skull to record the faint magnetic signals the brain produced as people typed. It reached 61% word accuracy on 90 hours of recordings, and kept improving as more data came in. It is still early days, but results like these are drawing serious money, from private investors and from governments alike. In the last six months, Chinese companies working on brain interfaces raised more than 4.6 billion yuan (nearly three times all of 2025). Meanwhile, Merge Labs raised $252 million to explore ultrasound as a way of reaching the brain without electrodes. The UK is putting £69 million into new ways of interfacing with neural circuits, and Japan has invested in an ambitious Moonshot program with the goal of freeing people from the limits of body, brain, space, and time by 2050.

By the close of the decade, non-invasive brain interfaces will become part of our daily lives. First, by turning deliberate intent into action, and limiting the need for translation layers like keyboards, controllers, and voice commands. Someone who cannot speak will be able to communicate with their friends and colleagues. Someone who cannot move will control household devices or prosthetics.

However, what really excites me isn’t command/control, it’s the ways that these interfaces will amplify our creativity, judgment, and memory, and improve our everyday lives. Interfaces that will help you access a memory that’s slipped away to time. Interfaces that let you catch an innovative idea at the edge of your mind before it’s gone.

Our brains only weigh 3 pounds. And the 86 billion neurons inside of them only require 20 watts to run. Yet, their capabilities far exceed the megawatt datacenters with millions of servers that are used to try to emulate them. The most successful technologies we can build will be those that amplify our unique human skills.
