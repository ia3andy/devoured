CI was failing. This was unexpected. I have dialed in my agentic workflow to a point where I would be surprised if one of my agents committed a branch that had failing tests. Maybe it's a CI flake or environment issue? Wait, it's SonarQube complaining about App.tsx? Did we pull in a new suspicious dependency from npm or something? Nope. SonarQube is flagging this file for cyclomatic complexity. Haven't seen that one in a while.

Yep. App.tsx consists of about 30 lines of code, except 15 of those lines are actually entire components that Codex decided to write each on a single line. That itself would be ok, just run it through prettier and we're done. But that doesn't explain the cyclomatic complexity. Each of these was a fairly involved component with a good amount of display and business logic. Old self would have carefully refactored it and made sure the code was readable. On the other hand, Codex didn't care that entire components were on a single line, and more importantly, it didn't mind the complexity. It had been happily and reliably iterating on that file through several features.

The CI tool was flagging an issue, perhaps correct, perhaps incorrect. But this is a real system so I had to dive in. After formatting the files, I went through and inspected the components and logic. Did Codex go beyond what a typical dev might have in terms of covering edge cases and did it slightly over-engineer it? Yes, but it was all correct. No complaints from me. All of the complexity was warranted by the business requirements. So I told Codex to make SonarQube happy, which it did by refactoring and splitting things up into 10 files. CI passed; things shipped. Although this particular incident had a quick resolution, you can probably imagine how it could have gone much worse. This was, after all, code I had never seen before.

This is where our industry is headed: Owning a software system will depend a lot less on having written it (or similar systems) by hand; instead, what will become most important is knowing just enough to know when something is wrong, and being willing and able to dive as deep as needed, when needed.

Divebots and golden nuggets

Imagine that you're on a boat (you decide what type of boat you like) and your job is to collect little nuggets of gold from the bottom of the ocean. Up until very recently, if you wanted to get your bag of gold you would need to jump into the water, swim to the bottom, look around, and stuff your bag. Easy peasy if you're in clear, shallow waters, but you can imagine how quickly this can escalate into something much more difficult. So as the shallow water mines started drying up, you and fellow engineers built all sorts of machinery and tooling to extract gold from ever deeper and more dangerous waters. This worked well for the most part, you and your team would carefully assemble, maintain, rotate the machinery and would constantly dive to the seabed mining operation to keep it running. Every so often you'd hear about the collapse of some mining operations, sometimes due to the sheer underwater pressure, other times through accidents, or operator incompetence. The challenge is that this is a very geologically active world, so the seabed keeps changing with some mines drying up and new ones popping up in different places.

Good news though! The last few years have seen the development of ever more sophisticated divebots that can go down and build a mining operation on their own. You just tell them where to build and off they dive and only surface to give you updates and gold nuggets. Things are great, you can now relax on your boat while your divebots do their thing and all you have to worry about is how much gold you can stuff into your boat. This is the Optimists camp.

Until...

"Wait till your divebots go too deep and get lost". "Haha your divebots brought you stuff that looks like gold but is actually shiny octopus poop". As the divebots proliferate you see more and more horror stories. Divebots getting lost and forgetting what they are supposed to be building. Divebots building something so complex and grotesque that they have trouble maintaining it, only for it to be discarded by the next generation of divebots. All of the underwater mining operations went from being carefully designed and engineered cathedrals to more like haphazard mansions, with hidden passages and horrors lurking. The more detached from the operation you are, the more spectacular the failures. This is the Skeptics camp.

Here Be Monsters

The software engineering industry (and I suspect many other industries soon) is living through a version of the above scenario. You probably already know whether Skeptic or Optimist describes you better, even if you're still not sure which camp you want to commit to.

The traditional wisdom is that in order to develop and maintain a non-trivial system you need to have a proper working model of it. I think few people would argue with that. And in software engineering you would acquire that working model by working directly with the code. Years and decades of practice writing and reading code give you the knowledge and tools needed to understand the systems you're responsible for. Every production incident, every late-night debugging session, every successful release. All of these experiences give you the perspective needed to have ownership of a system. In the context of this post, ownership means you're the one who gets called when something breaks.

Agents (divebots) are turning all of that on its head. Divebots produce output much faster than anyone can absorb, and while in complex systems of the past you could assume that the code had at one point gone through the hands of a human, that is no longer true. It is now possible for experienced practitioners to manage fleets of divebots and create software at an unprecedented scale. These operators know the craft and bring their considerable experience to manage and architect large systems that would have taken teams to build in the past. But that exposes what's probably the biggest challenge facing our industry: how do we train the next generation of engineers?

At their extremes, the Optimists and the Skeptics have wildly different answers to this question. On the one hand you have reputable voices saying that the only way to maintain control of these systems is to read every line of code the divebots produce. Be able to code it by hand. Be able to explain what every piece of code does and how it fits into the context of the applications. In the other camp, you have equally reputable voices saying to not even bother looking at the code. Focus your energy on figuring out what to build and forget about how it is built. The models will handle it. I don't think either of these is right.

The fact is that the way that today's senior engineers learned, ie. through coding by hand, endless hours debugging, production incidents, etc. is not a path that is viable for juniors today. To be clear, it is not economically viable, meaning that it is highly unlikely that companies will be willing to sponsor juniors for years while they "learn to do it by hand". That's wishful thinking. An alternative that is often presented is the "make sure you understand every line your agent wrote" advice, or "pair with a senior", or "perform careful code reviews". Again, wishful thinking at all but perhaps a few shops. If you think that the industry at large will adopt these practices, I've got a bridge to sell you. Hit me up.

In order to figure out a viable path for achieving some measure of mastery, we have to look at what it means to own a non-trivial software system. If you think about your typical architect/lead/CTO and how they operate, it becomes clear that to be successful they need to have a model of the system in their head, a chart of the waters. The real question is at what level of granularity? Imagine a legacy 1-million line codebase, it was written and expanded over the years by a number of people. The CTO may have even written parts of it. Now imagine there is a production issue and you're the unlucky SRE trying to figure this out at 2AM while getting angry texts from people in different timezones. What chart of the system do you need? You need a high-level, far-away map of the entire system, and you need to know just enough to know where you need to dive in, where the monsters live, and you need to be able to jump into any unknown codebase, figure out what's happening, push a hotfix, and describe what happened clearly in your incident report.

Note that this is not detailed knowledge of the whole system. The only way we can manage complex systems is by building up abstractions. Each layer lets us build a bit more, but whatever map you have, it is important to remember that it is abstractions all the way down, and no one really understands all the layers. There is a big difference between "I wrote module X and know it inside out" and "I have an appropriate model of the system abstractions and can dive into any module and fix it when it breaks". Coding by hand gives us the former, but the owner of a system, the person who gets called, needs the latter.

So how does this picture get built? And how can someone be expected to dive into unknown waters and fix the mess the divebots made if they've never mined gold nuggets by hand?

Fundamentals, Breadth, and Just-in-Time Depth

Some of the most insidious failures in complex systems are quiet failures. The sort where things mostly work, but there is something off about the system. Maybe it's suddenly slower, or error rates went up, or it works perfectly most of the time but fails every 19th attempt. Not to the point where you're getting paged at 2AM but enough that you get a feeling that something is off. As an owner of a system you need to understand the failure modes of the system. As an industry we have the tools to teach folks how to detect and reason about these failures: fundamentals. And the good news is that we've been teaching them to unwitting CS majors for decades. I'm talking about data structures & algorithms, networking, concurrency, distributed systems, etc. It doesn't have to come from a formal education either, these are topics you can read about and learn on your own.

To paint a simple picture (if you're a senior engineer please bear with me here), imagine you send your divebot on a mission. You're in shallow waters and you send it 2m underwater to collect some gold nuggets. It comes back in 4s and all is good. Next, you send it down 4m. This time though it takes 16s. That's odd. The next mission is 16m down, and it takes 256s. You get the picture. Something is obviously off. In computer science this would be called quadratic complexity, and probably the best way to recognize it is to understand the theory and know that such a thing exists.

Which brings me to the next pillar of ownership: breadth. Most of the successful engineers and engineering leaders I have had the pleasure of working with were able to hold a discussion on almost any topic, whether technical or not. They may have been specialists in certain things, but what was most impressive was their ability to draw upon and weave together seemingly disparate concepts from different disciplines and use that knowledge to solve problems. This is the high-level map of the waters. This is what tells you certain seas are more dangerous than others. The breadth of knowledge is what enables you to say "let's dive here". And to be clear, I'm not talking about expert level knowledge in every domain imaginable, but just enough to know what to look up when something is off. The buoys that guide your ocean exploration.

Someone asked me "how do we teach breadth? a survey class on everything?" and I didn't have a good answer at the time. But after thinking about it some more it seems to me that this is not something you can teach. There is no "Everything 101" you can take. The most we can do is encourage folks to read/explore/be curious. Absorb as much information as you can. Remember the weird incidents, the "oh crap" moments, think about how they connect to your work. And as you gain more experience, make a conscious effort to connect your everyday experiences with your work. I've lost track of how many times that one bit of knowledge I learned while working on a random project years earlier swooped in to save the day. This experience and breadth compounds, but it has to be maintained.

The final pillar of ownership is what I call just-in-time depth. This is the ability and willingness to dive into any problem and figure it out. Even if you're in uncharted waters. Even if you have to get in the cage and swim with the sharks for a bit. Even if you have to gather your team, load up the big proper submarine and dive down past the giant squids to figure out what's wrong with the system. This is the willingness to learn whatever is required to get the job done. It is also the humility to know when a dive is beyond your capabilities and you need to look for help. Not every dive can be done solo; not every dive is worth doing.

Some months ago, while working on my agent orchestrator, I was running into stability issues and lifecycle bugs with my agents and subagents. I knew what I wanted them to do, but the divebots that built the thing had done so rather haphazardly and all of the bugs were coming home at once. As I sat staring at my screen watching my agents crash for the nth time in an hour, and after several failed attempts at having the divebots fix their own mess, I recalled having read a Kubernetes book years earlier (yes I'm a nerd) and the words "level-triggered state machine" were suddenly staring me in the face. So I took a dive, deeper than I originally intended, even as far as the electrical engineering origins of "level-triggered". I brought my divebots along for the ride and re-designed the core architecture from the ground up. It has been rock solid since. This successful rewrite was mainly possible because I happened to remember something I had read years earlier, and was willing to dive in and learn whatever was needed to get the job done.

So you start with a high-level map of the ocean (system), and understand that it is layers of abstraction all the way to the bottom. And since all of those abstractions leak, your job as system owner is to know where to dive and how deep. It is no longer generalist vs specialist, or even the mythical T-shaped folks.

The new way of building things requires a generalist who can dive deep into the unknown, a "quick learner under pressure" and not in the resume-padded way. You will actually need to be able to learn quickly while fighting with sea monsters. I've interviewed many people over the years and when people put "quick learner" in their resume I like to ask them to tell me about a time they had to learn a complex system quickly, how did they do it? What would your answer be?

Owners of the Future

So fundamentals can be learned and breadth is gathered. Just-in-time depth, however, can be practiced. These three pillars are what enable someone to grow their ownership share in a system. Maybe you start in shallow waters, send your divebots down. You have solid fundamentals, decent breadth, so go build something. Just be ready to put on the suit and swim with the monsters when something breaks.

Who will be responsible for the sprawling underwater mining operations in the future? No one knows, but I would bet that it will be those individuals who are able to wield the three pillars above. It will be the kid who took an old radio apart and fixed it, without knowing the first thing about radios, just a desire to listen to music - even if they did end up with a couple of extra screws after putting everything back together.
