Introducing Fern

Fern is Mirendil’s first model. It is pretrained from scratch and supports a context window of up to 10M tokens. Its first version, Fern 0.1, was finished with a short round of post-training. New versions ship frequently.

Context is intelligence

A language model’s capabilities grow with compute, and there are two ways to spend more of it. One is the model’s parameter count. The other is its context length. A model with more parameters holds more static knowledge and can carry out more sophisticated reasoning steps. A model with a larger context window can use more task-specific information: draw on more of its own past experience, keep track of more specifications and instructions, integrate evidence from more documents and more code, and look at more data directly.

Fern’s architecture, training and serving can support up to 10M tokens, about 23 MB of code, at once. That’s about half a million lines of code, a codebase on the scale of the Megatron-LM library, dozens to hundreds of complete agent traces, or days of an agent’s work, all held in context at the same time.

Below, we first show that more context helps even with next-token prediction, the foundation of language model training and of every capability built on it. We then run a basic test of effective access to the long context with a standard benchmark (RULER). More comprehensive long-context results are coming soon.

What Fern is for

  • Works on larger repos: A 10M-token context window, so it can work across larger codebases.
  • Agentic coding: Runs commands, reads the output and keeps going until the tests pass.
  • Research code: Specialized for the code behind AI models: training loops, data pipelines and evaluations.
  • Debugging from logs: Works through long logs and stack traces to find where a run went wrong.
  • Analyzing full traces: Reads an entire agent or training trace end to end, so it can explain what happened and why.
  • Auditing training data: Scans a whole pretraining shard for duplicates, benchmark contamination and junk.

1 Bits per byte is a model’s cross-entropy loss per byte of text, so models with different tokenizers can be compared fairly. Lower is better.
