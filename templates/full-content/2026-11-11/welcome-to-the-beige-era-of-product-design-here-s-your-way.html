I’ll admit something: I can’t walk past a newly released car without studying its details: the headlight signature, the wheel design. These beautiful details deliver personality and character, while the rest of the car is becoming generic.

This phenomenon has a name: convergence. It isn’t new and it isn’t accidental.

Many have written about it. I like a few images that Luke Wroblewski posted recently, which show how products are becoming more and more similar.

As technology matures, it leaves behind the most optimised design. Which is, by definition, the one everyone else arrives at too.

Planes converge on a standard because of aerodynamics. Cars converge because of safety regulations. Phones and TVs converge on the most basic, intrinsic form because what’s on the screen is the thing people care about.

The same is happening in digital products, especially recently.

  • AI tools are trained on the same references and produce outputs based on probability: they reproduce the most common solution;
  • consequently, vibe-design tools have given birth to a plethora of similar products;
  • popular UI libraries like shadcn/ui have made apps wear the same outfit;
  • and optimisation culture has created safe winners (that look alike).

What does this tendency mean for us as designers? If everything looks the same, how do you stand out? How do you become memorable?

One answer lies where two of my most beloved design disciplines meet: product design and automotive design.

In automotive design there’s a concept called “jewellery”. It refers to the small, beautifully designed and well-crafted details (light signatures, a knurled dial, badging, a door handle, etc.) that elevate the car’s aesthetic and signal quality or luxury. Strip the badges off and most modern cars in each segment share more or less the same design decisions. Jewellery is where they start to feel different.

Digital products need the same thinking. Design teams need to figure out what their jewellery is.

Not decoration for the sake of it. Jewellery lives in the highly distinctive moments that deserve to be amplified through UX/UI. Moments where the experience should feel special.

Here’s an example I like from Deel. Time tracking is essential for pay-as-you-go contractors. It’s also, let’s be honest, one of the least glamorous tasks in anyone’s working day. Shahriyar M., who’s leading our wider mobile design effort at Deel, explored how the form factor of the iPhone Duo could change that: fold it into a tent on your desk, and it becomes a dedicated clock for your working hours. Timer front and centre, “pause” and “take a break” buttons, and that’s it. Plus a relaxing soft glow.

If you’re trying to find your own jewellery, a few things that help IMO:

  • Look for moments with emotional weight: the first payment received, a contract signed, etc.
  • Look at the mundane, repeated tasks. Make the boring delightful and it becomes memorable.
  • Be ruthless about quantity. A car with jewellery on every surface looks like a Christmas tree. Pick a few and protect them. Jewellery is the first thing to get cut when deadlines hit, so name these moments explicitly so they survive the roadmap.

Convergence isn’t the enemy. It’s the floor. Standardise what users expect so you can spend your energy where they don’t.
