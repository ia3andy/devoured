Interfaces in the Age of AI

People increasingly say that interfaces are becoming unnecessary: you can just ask AI for anything. What are we interface designers supposed to do, then? What should we teach students who want to build digital products?

A few thoughts.

First, this new reality did not catch the competent designers off guard. Interfaces aren’t exactly “becoming” unnecessary: they always were. In most cases, people only used interfaces because they had no other way to get what they needed from a computer. In the very first lecture of our User Interface Fundamentals course, we say that interface is evil, and that a designer’s job is to eliminate them.

Second, even people who had servants (assistants, slaves) always chose what to delegate and what to do themselves. People enjoy doing things themselves: it gives them pleasure, a sense of control, and a sense of authorship. They may not care how good their servant’s tools are, but for themselves, they’ll want the best. After all, the whole point of doing it yourself is the pleasure! People used to use interfaces out of necessity; now they’ll use them out of love. Interface quality will have to improve.

Third, look at how the interfaces of AI products themselves are evolving. Take ChatGPT: there’s Chat, Work, Codex, assorted sidebars, libraries, modes, a pile of switches. I have no idea what all this stuff is. It’s a complete mess, and they keep shuffling it all around. The limits and credits are impossible to make sense of, too. There’s enough work here to keep an interface designer busy for a lifetime. Judging by the results, the people building these products don’t even realise the depth of their own incompetence in interface design.

Fourth, suppose the future of your product is one where “agents” do everything. Have you tried designing an MCP server for a complex product? Deciding which actions an agent can perform, what they’re called, what data they accept, and what they return? That’s a hell of a design challenge. And to do it well, you need — surprise! — to understand users’ needs, tasks, and scenarios. The human users, not the agents acting on their behalf.

Interface design isn’t drawing buttons. If designers are finally going to start to realise that, I’m all for it.
