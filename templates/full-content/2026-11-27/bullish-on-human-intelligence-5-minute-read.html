Bullish on Human Intelligence

Many people argue that human intelligence is obsolete. Learning to think hard ourselves, especially on problems AI has already aced, is a waste of time. The only work left is to build RSI and prepare for AI takeover. And even this work should be done by burning thinking tokens from AI, not humans. I disagree.

It is perhaps non-obvious to the public, but frontier AIs — despite their success on Millennium Prize problems — are still bottlenecked by human talent in getting better. In particular, the bottleneck lies in one of the best capabilities of humans: sample efficiency. For example, good researchers can guess the principle behind a few datapoints, propose the experiment that most reduces their uncertainty about this principle, and eventually discover a principle that transfers to much larger scales or to very different problems. Each principle we find, and the process of finding it, makes us better at finding the next one. This compounding lets us grow exponentially by ourselves, without needing more resources.

People might argue human sample efficiency no longer matters. This is true in domains like math and TCS where verification is cheap and fast. But it still matters in domains like pretraining and biology where verification is expensive and slow. Frontier labs pay a few dozen researchers billions of dollars for their sample efficiency. And they are willing to spend 100x more building such sample efficiency into AI.

The best way to develop sample efficiency is to lead the problem-solving process yourself and burn your own thinking tokens. There is no shortcut: watching AI think is not thinking. I have worked extensively with automated AI researchers. Sometimes they solve my problems decently, and I understand their solutions. But in this era, solving any single problem is no longer very surprising. What I care about most is whether solving it makes me better at solving future problems. By this measure, I learn 100x more when I lead the research myself than when AI leads it. In retrospect, there are two main reasons:

  • I skip the most valuable part of research: making guesses and designing experiments.
  • The guesses and experiments proposed by AI are very off-policy to me: they are either boring or easy to predict. I learn the most from interesting things that I’m uncertain or confidently wrong about.

Sample efficiency is largely learned, not innate. I have worked with some really smart people, yet I’m still more sample efficient on research questions within my expertise. So developing sample efficiency is not a privilege; everyone can do it in their own domain.

Overall, I think humans should be cautiously RSI-pilled. Many properties of human intelligence are exactly the moonshot goals of frontier AI labs. Don't let RSI hack your brain into giving them up.

Counterargument: What if AI reaches human-level sample efficiency?

In short, labs’ current plan is to reach human-level sample efficiency in pretraining first, and then unlock general human-level sample efficiency in all domains.

There are many reasonable arguments about why labs could succeed. For example, pretraining is cleanly verifiable, scaling ladders can cut the costs of experiments, outcome RL can unlock high-quality CoTs even when we can’t verbalize why an idea would or would not work.

I expect some big, AI-proposed algorithmic breakthroughs in pretraining in 2027. But for human-level sample efficiency, assuming no slowdown or pause, I’m cautiously bearish on AI reaching it by 2030.

Even if labs succeed, the ability to think deeply and sample efficiently would still be the key to navigating the alien post-RSI world.

  1. Sample efficiency is also the foundation of taste and intuition.
  2. Arguably, to some people who are good at harness engineering, today’s AIs already reach human-level efficiency in these domains, as Levent Alpöge remarked when announcing his Riemann Hypothesis proof.
  3. With a small, constant amount of compute, they can propose ideas that make frontier AIs better every few months.
  4. It’s definitely fine to delegate implementation and small tweaks to AI.
